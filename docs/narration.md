# Narration — the full spoken script, chapter by chapter

`N` is the narrator (British male). `C` is the character voice
(British female) used for Ava's and Kit's interruptions.


# PART ONE — THE PAPER

## Chapter 0 — Two things that should not happen

**N** Here is a farmer. For four years running, his farm has lost money.

**N** Every year, more money went out of the farm than came in. And every year the hole got deeper.

**N** Anybody watching would tell him to stop. He does not stop. He keeps farming.

**N** And here is Nell. Nell owns a small factory, and she has the chance to build a second one.

**N** The sums say the new factory would earn her far more than her money is costing her. On paper, she should build it tomorrow.

**N** She refuses. Not this year. Perhaps not next year either.

**N** Nell makes the decisions. She owns the factory. Every choice in this film is hers, and she is sensible — not timid.

**N** Marshall carries the textbook. He always gives the standard answer. He is right when the world is calm, and badly wrong when it is not.

**C** And Ava asks the questions you would ask. She never lets Marshall get away with anything.

**N** We start from nothing at all. No economics is assumed. Not one word of it.

## Chapter 1 — What “investment” actually means

**N** Start with the simplest picture there is. A factory. Nell's factory.

**N** Every month, money comes in. Customers buy what she makes, and they pay her.

**N** Every month, money also goes out. She pays for materials, for electricity, for wages, for the rent.

**N** The difference between the two is what she actually keeps.

**N** Not all costs behave the same way. The rent is the same whether she makes one chair or a thousand.

**N** The wood is different. Make twice as many chairs and she buys twice as much wood.

**C** You may think investment means buying shares in a company. That is not what an economist means by it.

**N** It means this. You spend a large sum of money now, to build something that will produce revenue later.

**N** A factory. A shop fitted out. A well drilled. A machine bought. That is investment, and that is the decision this whole film is about.

## Chapter 2 — The textbook rule

**N** Marshall has a rule. It is the rule in every textbook, and it is worth stating fairly, because for most of the last century it was simply what everybody believed.

**N** Take everything she spends that moves with output — the wood, the power, the wages — and divide it by the number of chairs she makes.

**N** Now add a share of the cost of the building itself — the factory that had to be paid for before a single chair was made.

**N** This is the most important picture in the film, so we build it slowly. One line, running up the screen. Low down, the money coming in each month is poor. High up, it is good.

**N** Marshall's first half. If the money coming in clears the long-run average cost, then build. Expand. Enter the trade.

**N** Marshall's second half. If the money coming in drops below the average variable cost — if a chair no longer covers even the wood and the wages — then stop.

**N** Notice how close together those two lines are. Between them is a narrow band where the right thing to do is nothing at all. Hold that thought. It is going to get very much wider.

**N** One definition left, and it is the single most important one in this film. Nell needs a large sum to build. It comes from one of two places, and usually both.

**N** Either way, the money has a price. Blend the two together and you get one number.

**N** Earn less than that, and she would have done better leaving the money where it was. Every number later in this film is measured against it.

## Chapter 3 — Three facts that don’t fit

**N** Marshall's rule is clear, and it is wrong. Here are three facts that do not fit it. All three come straight out of the paper we are following.

**N** Here is what a firm's money costs it.

**N** And here is what firms insist a project promises before they will build it. Three or four times as much.

**N** Asked directly, firms named rates from eight to thirty per cent. The middle answer was fifteen. The average was seventeen.

**N** And the safe cost of their money at the time? A nominal rate of four per cent, and a real rate close to zero.

**N** Second. In the middle of the nineteen-eighties, American farmers stayed open through long stretches of losses.

**N** In nineteen eighty-three the average net income for a farm operator was six thousand dollars. For the year.

**N** Even leaving out the rent on the land and the mortgage payments altogether, it only rises to thirteen and a half thousand. For a whole family, for a year's work.

**N** Marshall's rule says: stop. They did not stop.

**N** Here is the dollar. Up sharply to the end of nineteen eighty-four — it rose by about fifty per cent — then falling from early nineteen eighty-five, until by the end of eighty-seven it was almost back where it had been in seventy-eight.

**N** And here are the imports. Flat for three years while the dollar climbed. Then rising — and still rising, long after the reason for it had gone away.

**N** The paper gives that level two different ways on two different pages. We use the first.

**C** They are not being stupid. The next four chapters explain why.

## Chapter 4 — The three ingredients

**N** Everything that follows rests on three things being true at once. Each one gets a picture, and each one gets an icon you will see again whenever it is in play.

**N** Nell pays for the concrete foundation of her new factory. The money leaves her hands, and the slab appears.

**N** Now she changes her mind, and tries to sell the foundation back. Nobody wants a hole in a field with concrete in it.

**N** And be careful. Sunk is not the same as expensive. A lorry is expensive and you can sell it again. A hole in the ground is cheap, and you cannot.

**N** Second ingredient. Nell does not know what next year holds. Will there be customers? How many? At what price?

**N** But the fog thins. Every month that passes tells her a little more than she knew the month before.

**N** Third. If she does not build this year, the chance is generally still there next year. The door stays open.

**N** This year. Next year. The year after that. Still open.

**N** Money you cannot get back. A future you learn about slowly. And a chance that keeps.

**N** When all three are true, waiting is worth money.

**N** And when any one of them is missing, it is not. Remember that — in chapter fourteen we take the third one away, and the whole conclusion flips over.

## Chapter 5 — Why waiting is worth money

**N** Put Nell exactly on Marshall's line. Exactly on it. The money coming in is precisely enough to cover everything, including the cost of her money.

**N** Marshall says she should be indifferent. Build, or do not build. It makes no difference either way.

**C** Hang on. There is a third thing she could do, and neither of those is it.

**N** Now watch what the future might do, starting from that point. Here are five futures. Nobody knows which one happens. Some end high. Some end low.

**N** In the two futures that end high, she looks, she sees the money is well above the line, and she builds. She gains.

**N** In the two that end low, she looks, and she does not build. She gains nothing.

**N** But look at that again, because everything turns on it. She gains nothing — and she loses nothing.

**N** Two outcomes that pay something. Three that pay nothing at all — but cost nothing either.

**N** Weigh each by how likely it is, and add them up. The answer is bigger than nothing. It cannot be anything else.

**N** Here is the sentence this chapter exists for. Waiting cuts off the bad half, and keeps the good half.

**N** That selective removal of risk is what waiting is worth.

**N** This is the most counter-intuitive part of the chapter, so I am going to say it twice. It has nothing whatsoever to do with disliking risk.

**N** Nell is assumed not to mind risk at all. She would take a fair gamble without blinking. And she still waits.

**N** The trade she is making is between risk now and risk later. It is about timing, not nerves.

## Chapter 6 — So how high is high enough?

**C** If waiting is worth money, why does she ever build at all? Why not wait for ever?

**N** Because waiting is not free. Every month she waits, she gives up a month of profit she could have been making.

**N** Here is Marshall's line again, where we left it.

**N** Marshall's line stays exactly where it is. It is not wrong about arithmetic. But the line where Nell should actually build slides up above it.

**N** And that gap has a meaning. It is the value of the chance to wait — the thing she destroys the moment she builds.

**N** Build now, and the chance to wait is gone for ever. She has to be paid for that, and the gap is the price.

**N** There is a mirror image of this on the way down, and chapter fifteen builds it properly.

**N** Marshall says: shut down when the money stops covering the day-to-day costs.

**N** The right answer is lower. She should absorb real losses before she gives up — because giving up throws away the chance of things getting better.

## Chapter 7 — A right, but not an obligation

**N** Forget factories for two minutes. Somebody gives Nell a voucher. It says: you may buy this bicycle for forty pounds, on any day you like. It never expires. And it never forces you to buy anything.

**C** Question. Is the voucher worth anything before she uses it?

**N** Of course it is. Somebody would buy it off her. It gives her a right she would not otherwise have, and it never obliges her to do anything at all.

**N** And here is the point. The moment she uses it, the voucher is gone. She has a bicycle instead. She cannot use it again, and she cannot sell it on.

**N** Which gives the rule, in one line. Use the chance when the holding premium has fallen to zero. Not before.

**N** Before that point, waiting is still worth something, and building throws it away for nothing.

## Chapter 8 — Putting numbers on it

**C** So far this is a story. Is the effect big enough to matter, or is it a curiosity?

**N** The paper answers that with arithmetic. There is mathematics behind it, and for now we leave it sealed in a box. Two dials go in. One number comes out.

**N** Dial one. How choppy the revenue is — how much the money coming in swings around in a typical year.

**N** The paper gives anchors. About ten per cent a year for revenue that moves with exchange rates. Twenty-five to forty for an oil well or a copper mine. The base case sits between, at twenty.

**N** Dial two is the cost of capital, from chapter two. What the money has to earn to be worth using.

**N** And out of the box comes one number. A multiplier. The money coming in must reach that multiplier, times the break-even level, before building is right.

**N** Base case. Cost of capital five per cent, choppiness twenty per cent. The multiplier is one point eight six.

**N** The money coming in must nearly double the break-even level before she should build.

**N** Said the other way round, that is a hurdle rate of nine point three per cent, against a true cost of capital of five.

**N** Now turn the choppiness dial up. Forty per cent a year — an oil well, a copper mine, a trade where prices swing hard.

**N** The multiplier jumps to three point three two. The hurdle rate to sixteen point six per cent.

**N** Now turn the other dial. Not up — down. Make money cheaper. Cost of capital two per cent instead of five.

**N** And the multiplier goes up. Two point six one. Cheaper money makes her more reluctant to build, not less.

**N** Say that again, because it is the strangest result in the paper. Cheaper money makes waiting more attractive.

**N** Here is why. When money is cheap, the future matters more to you. And if the future matters more, then finding things out about it is worth more. So waiting to find out is worth more.

**N** One footnote. If the revenue is also growing — say two per cent a year — two things pull against each other, and the waiting effect wins. The hurdle rate goes to thirteen point six per cent, and the trigger to three point nine times break-even.

**N** One last way of seeing all of that at once. The multiplier depends on two things and two things only. Two things in, one number out. That is a shape.

**N** Along one way, the revenue gets choppier. Along the other, the money gets cheaper. And upwards is a higher bar.

**N** And here is the shape.

**N** Look at the near corner. Calm revenue, expensive money. The sheet is almost flat, and the multiplier is almost one. That is Marshall's world, and in it he is right.

**N** Now follow it away from that corner. Every step towards choppier revenue, and every step towards cheaper money, lifts the sheet. The two effects do not cancel. They add.

**N** Hold that picture. In chapter thirty-five we come back to this exact sheet, and watch a firm walked across it — in the wrong direction.

**N** And that resolves the first of our three puzzles. Firms demanding fifteen per cent when their money costs them five are not being short-sighted. They are being correct.

## Chapter 9 — Money later is worth less than money now

**C** The next five chapters do the mathematics. Properly, and from the beginning. Nothing beyond square roots, fractions and percentages is used, and everything is built in front of you.

**N** A hundred pounds today, and a hundred pounds in a year. Which would you rather have?

**N** Today, obviously. Because you could put today's hundred to work, and have more than a hundred by next year.

**N** Put a hundred pounds somewhere paying five per cent, and in a year you have a hundred and five.

**N** Run that backwards, and it is a division. Divide by one point nought five, and you have turned money later into money now.

**N** A factory that earns five pounds a year, for ever. Here is the five pounds, arriving year after year.

**N** But each one has to be discounted back. Year one is divided once. Year two, twice. Year twenty, twenty times. So the far-off ones shrink away almost to nothing — and that is why the total does not run off to infinity.

**N** Add up every one of them, all the way out to for ever, and the sum is astonishingly simple. Five pounds, divided by nought point nought five. A hundred pounds.

**N** That is the only piece of machinery you need. The value of a stream that goes on for ever is the yearly amount divided by the rate. And the rate we divide by is the cost of capital.

**N** Hold on to the middle one. It is going to matter enormously in chapter thirteen.

## Chapter 10 — The textbook’s line, as a sum

**N** Nell is deciding whether to build. There are exactly two amounts, and we can write both of them down.

**N** What she gets: the money coming in each month, for ever, discounted back. From the last chapter, that is the yearly amount divided by the rate. Call the yearly amount R, and the rate the cost of capital.

**N** What she pays: the sunk cost of building it. Call that K.

**N** Three letters, and nothing more. R is the revenue a year. K is the sunk cost. And the third one we will only ever call the cost of capital.

**N** Subtract one from the other and you have what the project is worth.

**N** That is Marshall's line, and now you know exactly where it comes from. It is the interest on the money she sank into the building — the normal return on capital, and nothing more.

## Chapter 11 — How the future is modelled

**N** Every number in the next two chapters rests on one assumption about how revenue moves. Each year, revenue does one of two things. It goes up by a fixed percentage, or down by one.

**N** Percentages rather than pounds, because a big firm swings by big amounts and a small one by small amounts. That is the only realistic way to do it.

**N** Do that again the next year, and the next. Every point splits in two, and the possibilities fan out.

**N** Watch one possible path through it. That is one future — one thing that might happen.

**N** And here are a dozen more. Nobody knows which one happens. All anybody knows is the shape of the fan.

**N** Take a typical year and ask how far revenue moved. In a calm trade, not far. The outcomes bunch up.

**N** In a rough trade — oil, metals — they spread right out.

**N** The width of that spread is the number the paper calls the choppiness. It is the size of a typical yearly swing, as a percentage. Ten per cent with exchange rates. Twenty-five to forty for an oil well. And the base case at twenty.

**N** The paper writes it as a Greek letter. This film never will. It is the choppiness, and it is a percentage.

## Chapter 12 — The picture the paper draws

**N** The paper has two diagrams, and they are the heart of it. Across the bottom, the revenue coming in each year. Up the side, what the whole opportunity is worth to Nell.

**N** First, the straight line. If she builds today she gets the revenue for ever, divided by the rate, minus the sunk cost. That is the sum from two chapters ago, and drawn against revenue it is a straight line.

**N** It starts below the axis, because at zero revenue she would simply lose the sunk cost. And it crosses zero exactly at Marshall's line.

**N** Now the second line, and this one is a curve. It is what the chance to build is worth if she does not use it yet.

**N** They meet here. And they do not cross — they touch, and run off in the same direction.

**N** And there, between where the textbook says build and where she actually should, is the gap this whole film is about. You have now seen exactly where it comes from.

**N** The paper finds that touching point with calculus. We are about to get exactly the same answer with a square root.

## Chapter 13 — The one formula in the film

**N** Start inside. Eight, times the cost of capital, divided by the choppiness multiplied by itself.

**N** Add one to it. Take the square root. Add one again, and halve it. That is the whole formula.

**N** The paper calls the answer beta. This film calls it the steepness, because that is what it measures — how steeply the value of waiting curves upward.

**N** The paper prints two point one five. That is your answer, with the paper's own rounding.

**N** Divide the steepness by itself-minus-one.

**N** Two point one five eight, divided by one point one five eight. One point eight six. There is the multiplier from chapter eight — and you have just derived it.

**N** Multiply the textbook's line by it, and you have the real trigger.

**N** Or multiply the cost of capital by it, and you have the corrected hurdle rate. Nine point three per cent.

**N** And now you can see why both dials push the same way. More choppiness, or cheaper money, both make the number inside the square root smaller. That makes the steepness smaller. And a smaller steepness gives a bigger multiplier.

**N** One honest note to finish on. All of that works because the project, once built, always makes money. Once you let it make losses and allow her to give up, the two lines have to be found together, and there is no formula — it needs four equations and a computer. The paper does that, and prints the answers: nought point seven two, and one point six two.

## Chapter 14 — When this is wrong

**N** Being honest about the limits is part of the teaching, and it also stops you over-applying the idea. The paper sets out four qualifications.

**N** Everything so far assumed the chance was Nell's alone. Suppose it is not. Suppose there is one site, or one licence, and several firms want it.

**N** Nell hesitates, sensibly, to see how things develop.

**N** And somebody else walks through the door.

**N** When the chance can be snatched, waiting is not possible. And when waiting is not possible, the textbook's trigger is valid again. This is a real limit, and Part Three comes back to it.

**N** Sometimes moving first has a value of its own — you take the best position, and everyone else has to work around you. That pulls the other way.

**N** The right answer balances the two against each other.

**N** Six firms, each looking at the same opportunity, each with slightly different information about it.

**N** Each one looks around, sees that nobody else has moved, and concludes the others must have found something discouraging. So each revises its own view downwards.

**N** And so everybody waits. Not because the opportunity is bad, but because everybody is reading everybody else's silence.

**N** Then one of them moves.

**N** And now everybody revises upwards — because that firm must have seen something good. Investment arrives in a sudden bunch. Which is why it comes in waves.

**N** This one comes with a quotation, because it is the single idea Part Three leans on hardest. Dixit is quoting Bernanke.

**N** In plain words. When you can wait, it is mainly the bad possible futures that decide whether you build now.

**N** Because the good futures will still be there next year. You have not missed them. Waiting only ever protects you from the bad ones.

## Chapter 15 — When to give up

**N** To make the point sharp, the paper assumes that idle machinery rusts. Totally, and immediately. So pausing and quitting become the same thing, and starting again means paying the whole sunk cost over again.

**N** And the paper flags that itself, as a deliberate simplification. Where a firm really can mothball a factory and restart it cheaply, this half of the argument gets weaker. Hold on to that — Part Three needs it.

**N** Suppose the building costs more to put up. A bigger sunk cost. What happens to the two lines?

**N** The build-line goes up. She is more reluctant to start.

**N** And the quit-line goes down. She is more reluctant to stop.

**N** Say that one twice, because it is not obvious. A bigger stake to keep alive makes her slower to start and slower to stop. Both at once, and for the same reason — there is more to protect.

**N** One more. If leaving is itself expensive — redundancy payments, restoring the site of a mine — then the build-line rises further still. She is more cautious about entering something she may have to pay to escape.

## Chapter 16 — The zone of inaction

**N** Take away the uncertainty for a moment, and leave only the sunk cost. The textbook still has a small band where doing nothing is right — from one, to one point one.

**N** That is the whole of it. A band nought point one wide.

**N** Now put the uncertainty back in.

**N** Nought point seven two, to one point six two. Nought point nine wide.

**N** About nine times wider. That comparison is ours, not the paper's — the paper prints the four levels and calls the difference quite a dramatic difference. We did the division.

**N** This band has a name. The zone of inaction. The stretch where the right thing to do is nothing at all.

**N** First. Small frictions can produce much larger rigidities than models which ignore gradually-arriving information would ever suggest. A little stickiness goes a very long way.

**N** Second, and this one reaches outside the firm altogether. Hiring and firing cost money. So exactly the same logic applies to jobs.

**N** And the paper draws a conclusion from that which is worth hearing. The popular worry about loss of jobs — the one economists usually wave away — may have more justification than the textbook allows.

## Chapter 17 — Hysteresis

**N** She starts at one. Money coming in exactly covers the day-to-day costs. Nothing is built.

**N** It rises. It crosses one point one — the textbook's build-line — and nothing happens. She does not build.

**N** It keeps rising. And at one point six two, she builds.

**N** Now the money coming in starts to fall. Back past one point six two. Back past one point one.

**N** All the way back down to one — exactly where it started.

**N** The cause has completely reversed. The effect has not.

**N** Take an iron bar, and loop a wire around it.

**N** Pass an electric current through the wire, and the iron becomes magnetic.

**N** Now switch the current off.

**N** The magnetism does not go away. Some of it stays. The cause was temporary. The effect lasted.

**N** And the paper's own careful point. Sunk costs alone can produce some of this. Adding uncertainty magnifies it dramatically.

**N** The dollar rose, and foreign firms poured into America — years late, because they were waiting to be sure.

**N** Then the dollar fell back to where it had been. And they did not leave. They had paid to get in, and leaving would mean paying to get back in again later.

**N** The cause reversed. The effect stayed. That is all hysteresis is — and now you have watched it happen twice.

## Chapter 18 — Two countries

**N** Here is Nell, in America. And here is Kenji, running a similar factory in Japan.

**N** American firms of the period demanded very high returns before they would build — and then abandoned whole fields after short stretches of losses. Colour televisions. Video recorders. Semiconductors.

**N** Japanese firms did the opposite on both counts. They invested aggressively, and they hung on.

**N** Remember what uncertainty does to the two lines. It raises the build-line and lowers the quit-line. Together. It cannot do one without the other.

**N** So a firm too hesitant to invest should be more willing to ride out bad periods, not less. The American firms were the opposite on both counts. The story does not fit.

**N** Here is the spread of possible futures for a firm. Bad ones to the left, good ones to the right.

**N** For the Japanese firms, the bad half was cushioned. Government support, and cartels tolerated in recessions, cut off the worst outcomes.

**N** Now use the bad news principle. Waiting is worth less when there is less bad news to wait out. So they entered early.

**N** And because the upside mattered relatively more for them, they stayed late as well. One lopsided distribution, both puzzles solved.

**N** Which gives two rules, and they are probably the most practically useful sentences in the whole article. To get firms to invest sooner, reduce the downside risk.

**N** To stop firms leaving, improve the upside.

**N** Different instruments, for different jobs. Remember that. Part Three is built on it.

## Chapter 19 — What a whole industry looks like

**N** Six similar firms, all facing the same swinging demand.

**N** When the price rises to the entry line, new firms come in. More supply, and the price stops rising. So it never gets above that line.

**N** And when the price falls to the exit line, firms leave. Less supply, and the price stops falling. So it never gets below that one either.

**N** With these particular assumptions, the market's two lines turn out to be exactly the ones a single firm on its own would have chosen. So every number from chapter fifteen carries straight over.

**N** Carry the caveat, because it matters later. That result holds for identical, small, price-taking firms, under the paper's particular assumptions. It is not a general truth.

**N** So what should we expect to see? In the paper's own words: significant periods of supernormal profits with no new entry, and of operating losses without exit.

**N** An industry sitting perfectly still. Profitable firms not expanding. Loss-making firms not closing. And nothing wrong anywhere.

## Chapter 20 — Outside business

**N** The paper closes with an observation that reaches well outside business. Many personal, social and political decisions have exactly the same three features. Costly to reverse. Made under uncertainty. And the timing is yours to choose.

**N** So the same inertia applies to them too.

**N** A man and his partner, in New York, each with a rent-controlled flat. Their relationship reaches the point where she suggests they give one of them up.

**C** Let us give one of them up.

**N** And he explains to her, at length, the importance of keeping options alive. It is unlikely they would split up, he says, but given a positive probability, and so on.

**N** She took it very badly, and ended the relationship.

**N** Financial economists who hear that story say it proves how right he was. The paper says something better. He had misread the situation entirely. It was not a decision problem under uncertainty at all.

**N** And that gives the chapter a real point rather than a joke. It shows a limit of the whole framework. Option value is not always the right lens. Sometimes the irreversibility is the message.

## Chapter 21 — Everything, in order

**N** Give up at nought point seven two. Build at one point six two. And in between, a wide stretch where the right thing to do is nothing.

**N** The farmer keeps going because closing is final. Staying open keeps the good years possible, and that is worth more to him than the losses are costing him.

**N** And I do not build, because building is final too. Waiting another year is worth something, and this year's numbers are not yet good enough to pay for it.

**N** One more thing before we stop. A government somewhere spent hundreds of billions of pounds on a policy that assumed none of this was true.


# PART TWO — THE POLICY

## Chapter 22 — Money, and who is in charge of it

**N** Nothing here assumes you know what a central bank is, or an interest rate, or a bond. We build all of them from scratch, exactly as we built cost and revenue.

**N** And there is one new person. This is Kit. He is a student, and the argument in Part Three is his. He notices things, gets excited, and then talks himself down when the evidence will not carry him. That last part is the whole point.

**N** Borrow a hundred pounds at five per cent, and you pay back a hundred and five after a year.

**N** Meet the Governor. He runs the central bank. He is well-meaning, he holds the levers, and he is genuinely trying to help. He is not the villain of this film. There isn't one.

**N** After the crash of two thousand and eight, the Bank wanted people and businesses to spend and build. So it did the usual thing. It cut the rate.

**N** And it cut it again. And again.

**N** And by two thousand and nine the rate was almost at zero. The lever had hit the floor. You cannot cut much below nothing.

**N** So they needed something else. That something else is the subject of the next two chapters.

## Chapter 23 — Why cutting the rate was not enough

**N** In normal times the Bank's rate travels down this line, through a bank, and comes out as the rate an ordinary firm pays.

**N** Then, in two thousand and seven and eight, banks stopped trusting each other. Lenders would provide funds to a bank only at a much higher price — if at all.

**N** A wedge opened up. The Bank's rate went down. The rate a firm actually paid did not follow it.

**N** And note that they stopped just above zero, not at it — because of worries about what extremely low rates would do to banks' own profits, and to the working of the money markets themselves.

**N** All of that together goes by one name. Unconventional monetary policy.

## Chapter 24 — What quantitative easing actually is

**N** This is the chapter where it would be easiest to lose you, so we go slowly, and we do the key sum three times.

**N** A government wants to spend more this year than it collects in tax. So it needs to borrow.

**N** And here is somebody with money they would like to put somewhere safe — a pension fund, an insurer, a saver.

**N** The saver hands over a hundred pounds.

**N** And gets back a piece of paper. A promise. Five pounds every year, and then the hundred pounds back on a fixed date.

**N** Year after year, the five pounds arrives. It never changes. That is the whole point of it.

**N** And on the date printed on it, the hundred pounds comes back.

**N** The saver does not have to keep it until then. There is a market, and the gilt can be sold on to somebody else, at whatever price the two of them agree.

**N** But whatever price it changes hands at, the five pounds a year printed on it never changes. That one fact is the whole of the next section.

**N** The payments printed on the ticket never change. Whatever happens, it pays five pounds a year. Hold on to that.

**N** Price up, return down. Always. There is no way round it, because the payments on the ticket are fixed.

**N** The central bank creates new money.

**N** And uses it to buy these tickets. In enormous quantities.

**N** So many buyers appear that the price of gilts rises. And therefore, by the sum we just did three times, the yield falls.

**N** One careful sentence about creating money, because it is the thing everyone misunderstands. The Bank credits the seller's account with money that did not exist before, and takes the bond in exchange.

**N** Nothing is printed, and nothing is given away. An asset is swapped for money.

## Chapter 25 — Three sets of books

**C** The policy is easy to describe and easy to get wrong. So we are going to follow the money through three sets of books, one at a time.

**N** The people who actually sell the gilts are usually not banks. They are pension funds and insurance companies — the non-bank private sector.

**N** The seller hands over its gilts. So its holdings of gilts go down.

**N** And it gets money instead — not printed notes, but a number credited to its bank account. So its deposits go up.

**N** The central bank now owns the gilts. And it pays for them by creating reserves — money that only banks hold, at the central bank.

**N** And the bank in the middle sits between them. It has more reserves on one side, and it owes the seller more deposits on the other. Both sides of its books grow together.

**N** Look at that and notice what has not happened. Nobody has been given anything. Every single one of those entries is matched by another one.

**N** What has happened is that the seller's portfolio has been disturbed. It wanted gilts, and now it is holding money. And that disturbance is where the whole mechanism starts.

**N** Ordinary monetary policy sets the price of money. One short-term interest rate, and everything else follows from what people expect that rate to do.

**N** Quantitative easing sets the quantity instead. And it aims directly at longer-term rates, rather than reaching them through expectations.

**N** And the authors are careful here. There is nothing unusual about a central bank buying assets at all. What distinguishes these operations is the circumstances they took place in, and their scale.

**N** And one more difference, which matters enormously in the next chapter. What is bought. Short-dated government debt is very nearly the same thing as money.

**N** Long-dated gilts, company debt and mortgage-backed securities are not. The effectiveness of the policy may depend on what is bought, as well as how much.

## Chapter 26 — Channel one: the hot potato

**N** Leg one of the chain has three channels, and this is the big one. The one the Bank of England itself puts first.

**N** At the zero lower bound, money pays nothing. And a piece of government debt that matures next month, at a rate of practically zero, also pays nothing.

**N** If the seller does not care which of those two it holds, the story ends right there. It takes the money, puts it in a drawer, and nothing else moves.

**N** Economists have a name for that. And buying short-dated debt with money — what is sometimes called pure quantitative easing — can run straight into it. Japan did exactly that between two thousand and one and two thousand and six.

**N** Line up the things somebody can hold, from the safest at the bottom to the riskiest at the top.

**N** A ten-year gilt pays more than money. Sell it and hold the money instead, and the return on your whole portfolio drops.

**N** The seller has money it did not want. So it goes looking for something else to hold — something a little riskier, which is now relatively cheap.

**N** And whoever sells it that thing now has the money instead. So they go looking too.

**N** The authors call it passing the money around like a hot potato. Nobody wants to be left holding it.

**N** Think of all the interest-rate risk in the bond market as one pool, which somebody has to carry.

**N** When the central bank buys long-dated bonds, it carries some of that risk itself. So there is less left for everybody else to carry.

**N** And so the extra return they demand for carrying it falls, which pushes longer-term real interest rates down. That is the thinking behind the American programme nicknamed operation twist.

## Chapter 27 — Channels two and three

**N** The second channel does not need the money to go anywhere at all. It works purely through what the action tells everybody.

**N** A very large purchase is a signal. It says the central bank is serious about getting the economy going, and that it will keep rates low for a long time yet.

**N** And if everybody believes that, they act on it now, before anything else has happened at all.

**N** Which helps keep people's expectations of future inflation anchored. And expectations, as a later chapter will show, are themselves a lever.

**N** When markets seize up, you may not be able to find a buyer at all. So investors demand a higher return to compensate them for that risk.

**N** A central bank buying on a very large scale is, among other things, a buyer. It puts trading back into the market, and that premium comes down.

**N** There is also disagreement about how big those effects were and how long they lasted. And the effects on wider classes of assets are less marked than on company bonds.

## Chapter 28 — Leg two: cheaper money, and feeling richer

**N** Leg one moved the prices of things in financial markets. Leg two has to turn that into somebody actually spending money. And it does it in two ways.

**N** And that gives the sentence the whole policy rests on. Their words: this fall in the cost of capital should boost consumption and investment, by increasing incentives to borrow and reducing incentives to save.

**N** And be fair to them, because they do not leave it there. They name three further routes by which small firms might still benefit.

**N** An obvious implication of a fall in gilt yields is that the government's own cost of borrowing is now lower. Gilts are exactly what was bought.

**N** And their answer is that it will not change what the government does. Governments take a longer-term view. Their spending plans should therefore be unaffected by cyclical movements in interest rates.

**N** When asset prices go up, the people who own those assets are richer. And higher wealth should mean more spending.

**N** They put a number on it. Three hundred and seventy-five billion pounds of announced purchases will eventually boost British households' net financial wealth by about thirty per cent.

**N** Those gains went largely to the households holding the most financial assets — in particular older and more affluent ones. As with all monetary policy, they say, there are winners and losers.

## Chapter 29 — What banks cannot do with reserves

**C** There is a second way of telling the same story, and it clears up the thing almost everybody gets wrong about this policy. So it is worth four minutes.

**N** Narrow money is reserves — the money banks hold at the central bank. Nobody else can touch it.

**N** Broad money is deposits — the money in everybody else's bank account. That is the one that gets spent.

**N** And the transmission of this policy runs through broad money. Not narrow money. The Bank of England has said so explicitly.

**N** Put a pound of reserves into the system, says the textbook, and broad money rises by ten or fifteen times as much.

**N** In Britain, it was less than one.

**N** And the authors say plainly that this is not evidence the policy failed. On their own account, the right thing to expect was about one, not fifteen. The failure of broad money to explode is exactly what their theory predicts.

**N** Broad money grew by less than two hundred billion pounds during the first round.

**N** You will hear it said that the banks are sitting on all that money instead of lending it out.

**N** The banking system cannot do that. The amount of reserves in the system is decided by how the central bank chose to fund its purchases — not by any bank's own decision.

**N** Reserves went up a lot because of how the purchases were paid for. That is arithmetic, not a choice.

**N** The only way more reserves can lead to more lending is if they change a bank's incentive to lend — for instance by making the bank's own funding cheaper.

**N** Which is a completely different mechanism.

## Chapter 30 — The result that says none of it works

**C** The authors do something in this section that a less honest article would have left out. They set out, at length, a well-known result which says the whole policy does nothing at all.

**N** The seller hands over something risky and gets back something safe. Every channel so far has assumed that changes how much risk the private sector is carrying.

**N** Start with the risk sitting in the private sector.

**N** And households are the private sector. The risk has gone all the way round and come back to exactly where it started.

**N** So the household's risk, taken as a whole, has not changed. There is nothing to rebalance. And the policy does nothing at all.

**N** And the authors say it plainly. Those assumptions are very strong.

**N** There is one way out of it, and it is a promise. The central bank says it will hold rates low for longer than it otherwise would — even once things have recovered.

**N** And here is the catch, which the authors are careful about. The promise has to be believed.

**N** But once the economy has recovered — precisely because everyone believed the promise — the central bank has no reason left to keep it.

**N** A promise nobody has to keep is a promise nobody believes.

**N** Which is why one reading of quantitative easing is that its real job was the signalling all along — an action, rather than a promise, and actions are harder to take back.

## Chapter 31 — The other tools in the drawer

**N** Buying assets was not the only thing central banks did. The authors group the rest into three, and each one is worth knowing, because Part Three has to be fair about what else was going on.

**N** Left to themselves, people expect a rate that has been cut to nothing to start rising again fairly soon.

**N** So the central bank simply tells them otherwise. It says the rate will stay where it is for a long time yet.

**N** This one is the oldest job a central bank has, and it is not really monetary policy at all. When a sound bank cannot borrow simply because everyone has panicked, the central bank lends to it — at a penal rate, and against good collateral.

**N** And in the euro area, two operations in late twenty-eleven and early twenty-twelve lent over a trillion euros for three years — aimed, their central bank argued, at repairing the transmission mechanism itself.

**N** The third group exists because of the wedge from an earlier chapter. Banks looked risky, so their own funding was dear, so what they charged households and firms stayed high.

**N** The first was a guarantee. The government stands behind up to twenty billion pounds of bank debt, so an investor buying it faces the same risk as buying a gilt. And the bank gets that funding at a discount if it agrees to cut what it charges smaller companies.

**N** Though twenty billion is only about eight per cent of a year's lending to small firms by the largest banks. Limited in scale, as the authors put it.

**N** The second was bigger, and cleverer. Four-year funding, of at least five per cent of a bank's existing loan book — around eighty billion pounds across the eligible banks.

**N** And every extra pound a bank lent raised the amount it could borrow under the scheme.

**N** A bank holding its lending steady or growing it paid a quarter of one per cent.

**N** A bank shrinking its lending by five per cent paid one and a half — six times as much, on the whole amount.

## Chapter 32 — The chain

**N** Two economists, writing in twenty-twelve, set out how the policy is supposed to work. Christopher Bowdler and Amar Radia.

**N** They are careful, serious people, and nothing in this film is an attack on them. Their colour, from here on, is red.

**N** And the chain is the picture Part Two is built on, exactly as the vertical scale was Part One's.

**N** Why a chain? Because it is honest. Each link can be examined on its own.

**N** And if any single link fails, the whole thing stops there.

**N** The authors themselves split the chain into two legs. From the purchases to the price of assets.

**N** And from the price of assets to what anybody actually spends.

**C** And Kit wants to say this out loud, now, before anything else. He is only ever disputing the second leg. Not the first.

**C** The first leg is the part everybody argues about, and he is not arguing about it.

**N** And within that second leg, one link is going to matter more than all the others.

**N** Link five. The firm decides to build. Keep your eye on it for the rest of the film.


# PART THREE — THE ARGUMENT

## Chapter 33 — One sentence, two different kinds of claim

**N** Here is the sentence the whole of Part Two turns on. It is on page six hundred and twelve, and these are the authors' exact words.

**N** It contains two completely different kinds of claim, stitched together. Split it in half.

**N** The first half is a question about prices. Does the cheaper money actually arrive at a firm? You can go and check that.

**N** The second half is a question about behaviour. What does a firm do once the money has arrived? That is a different sort of question entirely.

**N** And be fair to them. These are honest, careful qualifications. They also name several other routes by which small firms might still benefit — through supply chains, through a cheaper currency helping exporters, and through banks passing on lower rates. All of that is in the article.

**C** Now Kit waits for the same thing to happen to the other half.

**N** One half of that sentence is hedged three times. The other half is not hedged once.

**C** His first reaction is not disagreement, and that matters for everything that follows.

**C** His first reaction is that two halves of one sentence are not the same kind of statement, and are being given the same amount of trust.

**N** This is not carelessness. It is what a certain kind of economics does by construction. It models markets in enormous detail, and it treats a firm as a machine. Numbers in. Decision out.

**N** There is nothing dishonest in that. It is a simplification, and simplifications are how anyone thinks about anything complicated.

**N** But it does mean that the last link in the chain is the only one nobody examines.

## Chapter 34 — The exception on page 613

**N** A page later, the authors ask a question about somebody else. What does the government do when its own borrowing gets cheaper?

**N** And notice what they open with. The government's borrowing cost is now lower. Gilts are exactly what the Bank had been buying. So if anyone got the cheaper price, the government did.

**N** The money definitely arrives.

**N** And nothing happens.

**N** Here is what the authors say, in their own words. Their spending plans should therefore be unaffected by cyclical movements in interest rates.

**N** And look at the reason. It has nothing to do with access. The money arrives.

**N** The reason is about the horizon the decision is taken over. Governments plan a long way ahead.

**C** Kit gets excited. They have admitted, he says, that a feature of how the decision is made can break the whole chain.

**C** And then he reads it again, and crosses his own conclusion out. That is the first of several times he will do that.

**N** That is a reason to go and look. It is not evidence about what you find.

**N** That sentence is the intellectual standard the rest of the film is held to. Everything Kit says from here has to meet it.

## Chapter 35 — Bringing Part One back

**N** Kit puts the two articles side by side, and notices they are arguing about the same quantity from opposite sides.

**N** One of them asserts that a lower cost of capital raises investment.

**N** And the other spends a whole article showing that the number standing between the cost of capital and the decision is not the cost of capital.

**N** The bar a project must clear is the textbook bar, multiplied.

**N** And the multiplier grows as the world becomes more uncertain.

**N** Which means we can go back to the sheet from chapter eight, and watch what the policy actually does to a firm's bar.

**N** Here is a firm in ordinary times. Twenty per cent choppiness, money at five per cent. Its bar is one point eight six times break-even.

**N** The policy makes money cheaper. So the firm slides this way — and the sheet under it goes up, not down. Two point six one.

**N** But the policy is only ever used in a crisis. So at the very same moment, the firm is dragged the other way too — into the choppiest part of the sheet.

**N** Both movements push the bar up. Not one up and one down. Both up.

**N** Which gives the sentence this chapter exists for. Quantitative easing is not a weaker version of a rate cut. It is a rate cut delivered in the one situation that blunts rate cuts.

**N** And be clear about whose is whose. The bar, and the fact that it rises with uncertainty, is Dixit's.

**N** Putting it next to this policy is Kit's.

## Chapter 36 — The instrument that fights itself

**N** Cheap money is meant to make building attractive.

**N** But cheap money also makes waiting cheaper.

**N** Because the cost of holding off for a year is the return the money would have earned meanwhile. Make that return small, and holding off hurts less.

**N** At a five per cent cost of capital, the multiplier is one point eight six.

**N** At two per cent, it is two point six one.

**N** Think of a firm's bar as two pieces. The break-even level, and the mark-up that waiting adds on top of it.

**N** The policy pushes the bottom piece down.

**N** And at the same time it fattens the piece on top.

**N** The direction is not in doubt. The bar does come down. The first effect wins.

**C** What Kit originally wanted to say was sharper. That it comes down by less than you would expect.

**C** He has withdrawn it. Because whether that is true depends entirely on how you measure it.

**N** Compare the two figures as percentage points, and the bar falls by more than the cost of money does.

**N** Compare them as proportions, and it falls by less.

**C** Both sums are correct. Neither of them is the true one. So he declines to pick.

**N** The claim he is entitled to is the plain one. Part of the policy is spent making patience more attractive.

## Chapter 37 — Two different kinds of fear

**N** Replay the bad news principle. When you can wait, it is mainly the bad possible outcomes that decide whether you build now.

**N** So the question to ask is: what does this policy actually compress?

**N** In financial markets, there is a price for carrying risk. Quantitative easing pushes that price down. Squeeze the spring.

**N** But the doubt inside a firm is a different thing. Will anyone still be buying in three years? Will this factory still make sense?

**N** Those are different worries entirely. A central bank buying government bonds reaches them only indirectly, if at all.

**C** So the honest version of his claim is about how directly, not about whether.

**N** What a central bank reaches directly is the price at which risk trades.

**N** What it reaches at one or two removes is the doubt itself.

## Chapter 38 — The concession that hurts most

**C** This chapter exists because Kit's own source damages him, and that is what intellectual honesty looks like.

**N** Replay the two countries. Japanese firms invested boldly and hung on through losses, because their downside was cushioned.

**N** Which half of that spread does a policy that props up prices and signals that the authorities will act actually work on?

**N** This half. Which is exactly the right half for keeping a struggling firm from closing.

**N** A policy that props up the upside is aimed at exactly the right half of the distribution for keeping firms alive. Which hands the policy a mechanism for the very thing he was going to explain another way.

**C** Why include something that damages you? Because it is what his own source says. The discomfort is the point.

## Chapter 39 — The evidence, read twice

**N** A third article, and a third pair of authors. Christopher Martin and Costas Milas. Their job was to gather up the studies of what the policy actually achieved. Their colour is green.

**N** Their conclusion is that the policy was effective at preventing a deeper crisis, and less effective at promoting recovery.

**C** The first time Kit read that, he treated it as a result. Here is what the numbers say. Write it down, move on.

**C** Reading it again, after Dixit, he cannot let it alone.

**C** Why would one policy be good at stopping a fall and bad at starting a rise? Those are not obviously different jobs.

**C** And he is uncomfortable. Not with the article — with himself, for having read that sentence once already and never asked whether there was a reason behind it.

**N** Preventing a collapse needs markets that keep working and the upside held up. And by the good news principle, that is exactly what stops a struggling firm closing. It is strongest when things are frightening, because there is most to repair and most upside to restore.

**N** Starting a recovery needs firms to commit money they cannot get back. By the bad news principle, that turns on the downside — which is exactly what fear makes them least willing to face. It is strongest when things are calm.

**N** So the policy is used in exactly the state where the first job is easiest and the second is hardest.

**N** It steadies without starting.

**N** And flag clearly which parts of that are Kit's and not Dixit's. That propping up the upside works best when things are frightening, and that the two jobs therefore pull apart, are steps Kit has added. Dixit's own text does not contain them.

## Chapter 40 — And the ones that did not come back

**N** You will recognise this picture instantly, because it is the hysteresis one. Same scale, same lines. Only the direction is different.

**N** A firm that is already running.

**N** The crisis arrives, and its takings fall.

**N** Down past the level where the textbook would have closed it. And still it holds on, because holding on keeps the chance of recovery alive.

**N** And then through the quit line. It closes.

**N** Now the policy works. Conditions come back up to normal. All the way back to where they started.

**N** The firm does not come back. Because coming back means paying the whole set-up cost over again.

**N** State it plainly. What the policy stops from happening does not need explaining.

**N** What needs explaining is why, for the firms it did not reach in time, nothing came back.

## Chapter 41 — The rivals Kit cannot beat

**C** Four doors. Kit opens each one, and cannot close it again. This is the chapter where the film earns its credibility, so none of these gets knocked down.

**C** All four are left standing open. Kit does not claim to have closed any of them.

**N** Which fixes the scope of the whole argument. It applies to investment a company can take its time over. Not to a race.

## Chapter 42 — The management half

**N** Grow Nell's factory into a large company, with several separately run parts.

**C** So say this explicitly, because it is easy to miss. Kit's dispute is not about how high the number is. It is about what kind of number it is.

**N** And here is the mechanism, as an actual queue of projects at the boardroom door.

**N** State the assumption openly, because it is doing a lot of work and it might be wrong. In a large company the thing that runs out first is not money. It is management.

**N** A minimum return set to solve that problem is tied to the next project in the queue — the best thing the company would have to drop in order to take this one on.

**N** And that is an internal quantity. It depends on how many good ideas the business is generating, and how many capable people it has spare.

**N** A fall in the return on government debt does not lengthen anybody's working week.

**N** The yield falls. And the queue does not move.

**C** So here is the version he can defend. Not that the number never moves. But that it moves on the company's clock rather than the central bank's.

**C** Changing as the pipeline of ideas and the stock of managers change, over years. For a policy meant to work inside a downturn, that is close enough to failure to matter. But it is not the same as never, and he will not say never.

**N** Treating a hurdle rate as a rationing device rather than a calculation is not an idea Kit invented.

**N** What he would claim is the use he has put it to.

## Chapter 43 — What is actually being claimed

**N** This is not a claim that quantitative easing does not work.

**N** Kit has not disputed that it moved prices in financial markets, which is the best-established finding in the whole body of research.

**N** It is not a claim that the theory is false.

**N** It is a claim that the theory is incomplete, in one specific place.

**N** So rebuild the chain one final time.

**N** Careful reasoning about what can go wrong at every stage.

**N** Except one. The stage where a quantity finally has to change, because somebody in a room has to say yes.

**N** The first is Dixit's, and Kit is mostly reporting it. When spending cannot be undone and the future is unknown, the bar is higher than the textbook says. And it rises as the world becomes more frightening.

**N** So a policy used in frightening times is working against the largest version of that bar. That is well-established economics, published in a general economics journal, and simply absent from the article he began with.

**N** The second is Kit's. And he holds it loosely, for all the reasons the four open doors gave.

**N** The gap is not a mistake in anybody's reasoning.

**N** It is a boundary.

**N** A model of the whole economy hands off to a decision it does not model. And the study of how that decision actually gets made sits in a different journal, on a different reading list, and in the other half of the same degree.

**N** And the last shot is all of them, standing either side of the chain, at the link nobody examined.
