# The Frictionless Firm

**A working paper on the last mile of monetary transmission**

Bowdler and Radia are careful about where the frictions in quantitative easing sit. Every one they name lies between the central bank and the price a firm pays for money. *None lies inside the firm that has to decide whether to build.* This paper argues that one missing friction is well established in economics and simply absent here, that a second is a conjecture belonging to management, and that the boundary the transmission literature does not cross runs between them.

| **Argues for** | Irreversibility, hysteresis and internal capital rationing |

## 1. The question, and why it is not a cheap shot

*Disagreeing with a paper is easy and usually worthless. The version worth writing down is the one where the paper's own text sends you looking. It is worth being clear that sending you looking is all a text can do: what you find has to stand on its own.*

Quantitative easing is supposed to work through a chain. The central bank buys government bonds. Bond prices rise and yields fall. Other asset prices move with them. Borrowing becomes cheaper. Firms and households, facing a lower cost of finance, borrow and spend more. Output and inflation rise.

Almost all of the disagreement in the literature is about the *first half* of that chain — whether the purchases really moved yields, by how much, for how long, and through which mechanism. That is where the event studies live, where the econometricians argue about one-day versus two-day windows, and where the portfolio-rebalancing-versus-signalling debate sits.

This paper leaves the first half alone. It takes the second half: the step from a lower cost of finance to actual investment. That step is stated in one sentence in Bowdler and Radia, defended in one paragraph, and qualified in three places — and all three qualifications are about the same thing. My argument is that a qualification they never make belongs there, and that it is missing for a reason worth noticing. Part of it is economics, published in a general economics journal, and simply on the other side of a specialism. Part of it is not economics at all, and lives in management.

> **The transmission mechanism is modelled with great care right up to the point where a firm decides, and at that point the modelling stops. The last mile of monetary policy runs through a boardroom.**

## 2. The theory under attack, stated fairly

The theory is the **cost-of-capital account of monetary transmission**. It is old, it is standard, and it is not peculiar to QE — it is the same logic that makes a conventional interest-rate cut work. It says: lower the rate at which a firm can finance a project, and projects that were not worth doing become worth doing, so investment rises.

> **PRIMARY SOURCE — THE THEORY**
> Christopher Bowdler and Amar Radia, *'Unconventional monetary policy: the assessment'*
> Oxford Review of Economic Policy, 28(4), 2012, pp. 603–621. Bowdler is at Oriel College, Oxford; Radia was at the Bank of England. Read on Oxford's Pathways to Banking programme.

Section III of their paper splits the transmission mechanism into two legs. Section III(i), *'From asset purchases to asset prices'*, contains portfolio rebalancing, signalling and liquidity. Section III(ii), *'From asset prices to spending'*, contains cost of capital and wealth. It is the second leg I am disputing. This distinction matters and I will hold to it throughout: nothing here is an argument about portfolio rebalancing.

Here is the claim in their own words:

> The rates at which households and firms access finance are typically related to the risk-free rates at the maturity that they seek to borrow. Therefore falls in the yield curve are likely to translate to a reduction in interest rates facing households and firms, in a similar vein to conventional monetary policy… **This fall in the cost of capital should boost consumption and investment by increasing incentives to borrow and reducing incentives to save.**
>
> — *Bowdler & Radia, p. 612*

Notice how much is doing work in the word *should*. The sentence has two halves. The first half — falls in the yield curve translate into lower rates facing borrowers — is a claim about *prices*, and it is testable, and the authors qualify it carefully. The second half — cheaper finance produces more investment — is a claim about *behaviour*, and it is asserted without qualification.

### ▸ What I thought reading it

My first reaction was not disagreement. It was that the two halves of that sentence are not the same kind of claim, and the paper treats them as though they were. The price half gets three qualifications across the next two paragraphs. The behaviour half gets none.

That asymmetry is not sloppiness. It is what a certain kind of macroeconomics does by construction: it models markets in detail and firms as optimisers. The firm is a function, not an organisation. Once the input changes, the output follows.

---

The three qualifications they do make are all about whether the cheaper price ever *arrives*:

> However, unlike in normal times, central banks turned to QE at a time when the banking sector was badly damaged. Thus, any channels of transmission through the banking sector were likely to be impaired.
>
> But households and smaller companies do not have access to capital markets, and so may not directly benefit from this channel of QE.
>
> …if the banking sector remains impaired for other reasons, perhaps because financial institutions are capital constrained, then reductions in their cost of issuing debt may have little impact on the price of credit for households and firms.
>
> — *Bowdler & Radia, p. 612*

All three are about access. A damaged banking system will not carry the cheaper price; a small firm cannot go round it by issuing a bond; a capital-constrained bank will not pass a lower funding cost through. Fair enough, and honestly stated. But all three leave the underlying theory intact. The implicit position is: *if the cheaper money reaches you, you will use it.*

## 3. The discipline: name your friction

There is a way of reading this literature that makes the gap visible, and it comes from the theoretical benchmark the whole field is organised around.

In a world with no frictions at all, quantitative easing does nothing. If markets are complete, if assets are perfect substitutes, if households see through the government's balance sheet to the taxes behind it, then swapping one government liability (a bond) for another (a reserve) changes nothing real. Private agents simply undo the swap. This is Wallace neutrality — a Modigliani–Miller theorem for open-market operations.

> **A note on attribution.** Bowdler and Radia call the irrelevance result *"first derived by Eggertsson and Woodford (2003)"* (p. 615). The origin is usually given as Neil Wallace's 1981 *American Economic Review* paper; Eggertsson and Woodford extended it to the New Keynesian zero-lower-bound setting. I have this from a secondary survey and have not checked Wallace's original, so I would not lead with it — but it is worth knowing that the lineage is longer than the paper suggests.

One caveat before I use it, because the discipline is easier to borrow than to apply. Wallace neutrality is a proposition about the *first* leg — whether an asset swap does anything to prices at all — and the frictions I go on to name are on the second leg, which only exists if the first has already been broken. So I am not naming a friction that breaks Wallace's theorem. I am borrowing the habit it enforces: say where in the chain your mechanism bites, and say it in a form someone could argue with.

The usefulness of the benchmark, so understood, is not that anyone believes it. It is that it turns every claim about QE into a claim of a specific shape. If you say QE works, you are saying that one of Wallace's assumptions fails, and you have to say *which*. Segmented markets. Limited arbitrage capital. Investors with preferred habitats. Impaired intermediaries. Demand for safe assets. Each of these is a named friction that breaks the neutrality result at a named point.

Bowdler and Radia do this properly. Their account requires that gilts and money not be perfect substitutes:

> If the private sector is indifferent between holding gilts and money — they are viewed as perfect substitutes — then the process ends there.
>
> — *Bowdler & Radia, p. 609*

And they name further frictions as they go: limits to arbitrage; investors' risk appetite; and — strikingly — regulatory constraints on the institutions that are supposed to do the rebalancing.

> …regulations facing pension funds and insurance companies — such as capital requirements and requirements to hold a certain share of sovereign debt — may limit their abilities to re-orientate their portfolios away from government bonds.
>
> — *Bowdler & Radia, p. 611*

### ▸ What I thought reading it

This is where the paper got interesting to me rather than merely informative. They are perfectly willing to put a friction *inside an organisation* — a pension fund's mandate is not a market failure, it is a governance constraint written by trustees and regulators. They even cite evidence that pension funds moved the wrong way, cutting equity allocations to their lowest level since 1974 in the middle of the programme.

So the paper is not naïve about organisations. It just stops being interested in them at the exact moment the money reaches a firm that might build something.

## 4. The observation that starts the argument

Line up every friction Bowdler and Radia name on the borrowing-cost route and they share a property. Segmentation, arbitrage limits, pension mandates, bank impairment, small firms' lack of capital-market access: all of them sit **between the central bank and the price a firm faces**. They are frictions in the plumbing.

Then, on page 613, comes the one exception that is not like the others.

> We would expect households and firms to increase their borrowing and spending in response to a fall in interest rates. But governments typically behave differently, taking a longer-term view. **Their spending plans should therefore be unaffected by cyclical movements in interest rates.**
>
> — *Bowdler & Radia, p. 613*

Here is an agent for whom the cheaper price definitely arrives. The government is the most creditworthy borrower in the economy, and gilts are the very instrument the purchases were aimed at; Bowdler and Radia open the paragraph by saying so — "an obvious implication of a fall in gilt yields is that the cost of borrowing for the government is now lower" (p. 613). And it is nevertheless expected not to respond. And the reason given has nothing to do with access. It is about the *horizon the decision is taken over*: governments plan long, so cyclical movements in the discount rate do not enter the calculation.

> **On the borrowing-cost route, this is the only exception in the paper that is not about access to credit. It is the only place where a feature of the decision itself — the horizon it is taken over — rather than a market failure, is allowed to break the link between a price and a quantity. And it is granted to exactly one agent.**

### ▸ What I thought reading it

I could not see what justified stopping there. If a long planning horizon is enough to insulate an investment decision from the discount rate, then the question is not whether firms are like governments — plainly they are not — but whether anything *in the way firms decide* does comparable work.

The paper does not ask. It is not an oversight so much as a boundary: asking would require a theory of how firms actually make capital decisions, and that is not what this paper is for.

---

Two points of precision, and the second cost me the strongest version of this section.

Note first the modal: *"should therefore be unaffected."* This is a modelling assumption, not a confession. Bowdler and Radia are not admitting a hole in their theory; they are applying a standard assumption about fiscal behaviour.

Second — and this is the objection I would have raised against myself if I had read the sentence more carefully — there is a reading of it on which nothing organisational is happening at all. "Taking a longer-term view" and "cyclical movements" together suggest the orthodox permanent-versus-transitory move: a long-horizon optimiser responds to the permanent component of a rate and ignores the temporary one. That is ordinary optimising economics, not a claim about how a decision gets made, and it is probably what the authors meant.

I think the observation survives that reading, but in a weaker form than I first gave it. What survives is that horizon — a feature of *when* an agent is deciding for, not of what it can borrow at — is allowed to sever the link between a price and a quantity, and is allowed to do so for one agent only. What does not survive is any claim that the sentence hands me an organisational friction. It does not. It prompted me to go looking for one; it is not evidence that one exists. Objection 1 concedes the same thing and I would rather concede it here as well, in the section that leans hardest on it.

There is also a rival buried in that reading that I ought to name now and answer later: if the distinction is permanent versus transitory, then the simplest explanation of why firms did not respond to QE is that they read it as transitory — a pure cost-of-capital story that needs nothing from Dixit and nothing from management. That is the last of the objections in section 9, and it is the one I cannot answer.

## 5. The alternative: investment as an option

> **SOURCE — THE THEORY I ALIGN WITH**
> Avinash Dixit, *'Investment and Hysteresis'*
> Journal of Economic Perspectives, 6(1), Winter 1992, pp. 107–132. Dixit is at Princeton; the article is his survey of the research programme he had been building through the 1980s, written for the whole profession and free to read at aeaweb.org. The framework is usually called real options theory. The book-length version, written with Robert Pindyck as *Investment under Uncertainty*, followed two years later. I have read the article in full, and quotations below are verbatim from the printed text.

The standard theory of investment says: compute the net present value of a project at your cost of capital, and if it is positive, build. Lower the cost of capital and more projects clear the bar. Dixit opens by pointing out that firms do not behave this way, and that the discrepancy is enormous:

> Firms invest in projects that they expect to yield a return in excess of a required or "hurdle" rate. Observers of business practice find that such hurdle rates are three or four times the cost of capital.
>
> — *Dixit, p. 107*

His explanation is that the standard theory quietly assumes a firm has only two options — build now or never build. In reality a firm usually has a third: *wait*. Dixit lists three features that make waiting valuable, and the argument needs all three of them:

> Three features are common to most investment decisions… First, almost as a matter of definition, an investment entails some sunk cost, an expenditure that cannot be recouped if the action is reversed at a later date. Second, the economic environment has ongoing uncertainty, and information arrives gradually. Finally, an investment opportunity does not generally disappear if not taken immediately; the decision is not only whether to invest, but also when to invest.
>
> — *Dixit, p. 108*

Waiting is not doing nothing. If the investment is irreversible (you cannot unbuild a factory and recover the money) and the future is uncertain (you do not know what demand will be), then the ability to wait and see is itself valuable. Committing destroys that option. So the project must cover not only its cost of capital but also the value of the option you give up by acting now:

> When these three conditions are present, waiting has positive value… But the "trigger" level of currently expected profit that makes it optimal to proceed exceeds the Marshallian normal return.
>
> — *Dixit, pp. 108–9*

The structure of his answer is what matters, and it can be stated without the algebra. The correct trigger is the textbook trigger *multiplied by a factor*, and in his base case that factor depends on two things: how uncertain the world is, and how heavily the firm discounts the future. (A footnote adds a third, the trend growth rate of revenue, and finds it usually widens the factor further.) Equivalently, a firm can carry on using the textbook rule provided it discounts at a marked-up rate rather than at its true cost of capital — what Dixit later calls an adjusted hurdle rate (the construction is on p. 115, the phrase on p. 117). This is the point to hold on to: in his model the hurdle rate a firm applies is *a function of the cost of capital*. It is the cost of capital wearing a mark-up. Section 7 will turn on that.

He then shows the mark-up is not a rounding error. On ordinary parameters — annual revenue volatility of 20 per cent, between the 10 per cent he cites for exchange rates and the 25 to 40 per cent he cites for oil and copper, and a cost of capital of 5 per cent — the factor is 1.86. Revenue must *nearly double* the level at which the project first breaks even before building is right, and the justified hurdle rate is 9.3 per cent against a true cost of capital of 5. At commodity-grade volatility the trigger is 3.32 times break-even and the hurdle rate 16.6 per cent (all pp. 116–17).

> To sum up, even when the cost of capital is as low as 5 percent per year, the value of waiting can quite easily lead to adjusted hurdle rates of 10 to 15 percent.
>
> — *Dixit, p. 117*

And the direction of the effect is exactly what this argument needs: **the mark-up widens as uncertainty rises**. Dixit states both ends of it explicitly. In calm conditions the option to wait is worth little, "option values become unimportant in this limit and the Marshallian criterion applies"; in turbulent conditions the multiple grows without limit and "Marshallian analysis becomes totally misleading" (p. 117).

### ▸ What I thought reading it

Two things struck me. The first was simply the reframing: I had always read a firm sitting on cash in a downturn as timidity or as a credit problem. Treating delay as an asset with a price makes it a rational response to information you do not yet have, which is a much less comfortable conclusion for policy.

The second is that this is a different mechanism from Bowdler and Radia's government exception, and I think that matters. Their reason is a long planning horizon. This reason is irreversibility under uncertainty. They are not the same thing, and I would rather say so than let the analogy do work it has not earned. What they share is only the structural feature: a property of the decision, not of the price, breaking the link between them.

There is a third thing, and it took me longer to notice. Dixit's paper and Bowdler and Radia's are arguing about the same quantity from opposite sides. Bowdler and Radia assert that a lower cost of capital raises investment. Dixit's whole article is a demonstration that the number standing between the cost of capital and the decision is not the cost of capital.

### The comparative static that cuts hardest

There is a second-order result here that is easy to miss and that I think is the sharpest thing in the argument. The mark-up depends on the discount rate as well as on uncertainty, and it moves the *wrong way*. A lower discount rate makes the future loom larger; a future that looms larger makes information about it more valuable; and waiting is how you buy that information. Dixit says it plainly — "when the future is less heavily discounted, the value of waiting for more information goes up" (p. 117) — and his own two calibrations show it. At a 5 per cent cost of capital the mark-up is 1.86 times. At 2 per cent it is 2.61 times.

So a cut in the cost of finance pushes on the trigger in two directions at once. It lowers the bar a project must clear. And it raises the multiple by which that bar is marked up. The first effect is the larger of the two, so the bar does come down — but it comes down by less than the cut, because part of the cut is spent widening the wedge it has to cross.

That is a strange property for a policy instrument to have, and it is worth saying why it arises rather than treating it as a curiosity. Cheap money is supposed to make building attractive. But cheap money also makes *not building* cheaper, because the cost of holding out is the return you forgo meanwhile. An instrument that lowers the price of capital lowers the price of patience at the same time.

I should be careful about the size of the claim, and about the measure. The sign is not in doubt: within Dixit's model the bar does come down when finance gets cheaper. What is damped is the *proportional* movement — the bar falls by a smaller fraction than the rate does. Section 7 reads the same two calibrations in percentage points and gets a different-looking answer, and I flag that there rather than let the two passages look like a contradiction. Part of the instrument works against itself, and that is interesting enough without dressing it up as ambiguity.

### Hysteresis: the effect that outlives its cause

The half of Dixit's paper that gives it its title is about what happens after the firm has built, and it is the half that matters most for a policy that is trying to restart something.

Allow a project to make operating losses and the same logic runs in reverse. A firm should not close the moment revenue falls below its running cost, because staying open keeps alive the option of recovery. So there is a low trigger for quitting as well as a high trigger for building. Dixit calls them L and H, and lines all four levels up in one ordering (his equation (8), p. 120): the quitting trigger L sits below the running cost, which sits below the textbook entry level, which sits below the true entry trigger H. Waiting pushes the two real decision points *outward* from the two textbook ones — the firm builds later than the textbook says and quits later too.

Between L and H nothing happens at all. Dixit calls this the zone of inaction, and on his own base-case numbers it is startlingly wide: with a running cost of 1 and a sunk cost of 2, the textbook band runs from 1 to 1.1 while the option-value band runs from 0.72 to 1.62, a difference Dixit calls "quite a dramatic difference" (pp. 120–1). Nine times the width, on his figures — the ratio is mine, the numbers are his.

Now push a firm's revenue up through H, so it builds, and then let revenue fall all the way back to where it started. The firm does not close, because the starting level is nowhere near L. The cause has reversed; the effect has not.

> The cause (the current) was temporary, but left some lasting effect (the magnetized bar). This phenomenon is called hysteresis, and by analogy the failure of investment decisions to reverse themselves when the underlying causes are fully reversed can be called economic hysteresis.
>
> — *Dixit, p. 122*

And it runs both ways. Dixit is explicit that once a firm has crossed L and quit, a recovery does not undo it: "a subsequent rise back to 1 in revenues will not restore the project; there is hysteresis in the reverse direction, too" (p. 122).

His worked empirical case is the other direction — entry that would not reverse. The dollar rose about 50 per cent in real terms to the end of 1984; foreign firms took until 1983 to begin their sustained entry into the US market; the dollar then fell from early 1985 until by the end of 1987 it was "almost back to its 1978 level"; and import volume "did not decrease for another two years; if anything it rose a little" (pp. 107–8, revisited at p. 122). That is a cause that reversed and an effect that did not, which is the shape I need, but it is firms staying rather than firms failing to return. (Dixit's two accounts of the endpoint are not quite consistent — p. 108 has the dollar almost back to its 1978 level, p. 122 has it falling back to the 1980 level. Since he measures the rise from 1980, either reading implies the whole rise was given back; I note the slip rather than lean on it.)

One assumption should be flagged here, because the strongest limb of my argument later depends on it. Dixit gets his clean result by supposing that idle machinery rusts totally and instantly, so that any pause counts as abandonment and restarting costs the whole sunk cost again (p. 119). He says he is doing this "to highlight this feature" and points to McDonald and Siegel (1985) for the case where suspension is genuinely temporary. The exit half of hysteresis is therefore at its strongest in his model and weaker wherever a firm can mothball rather than scrap.

> **A wide zone of inaction is what turns a temporary shock into a permanent one. It is also what makes a policy that restores a price fail to restore the quantity that price was supposed to govern.**

## 6. Why this is a claim about QE and not about interest rates

There is an obvious objection that I need to meet before going further, because if it holds, the argument is much less interesting than it looks.

**The objection:** if investment is insensitive to the discount rate, that is a problem for *all* monetary policy, not for QE specifically. It refutes the interest-rate channel in general. So what has been said about QE?

The answer is that real options does not say investment is insensitive to the discount rate. It says the sensitivity is **state-dependent** — high when uncertainty is low, low when uncertainty is high. This is not a gloss on Dixit; it is the structure of his result. Double the volatility in his base case, from 20 to 40 per cent, and the required mark-up over break-even goes from 1.86 times to 3.32 times (pp. 116–17). At the calm end the mark-up collapses to one and, in his words, "the Marshallian criterion applies"; at the turbulent end it grows without limit.

The reason is worth stating in words, because it is the whole argument in miniature. A cut in the cost of finance lowers the bar. Uncertainty raises it. When both move together, what a firm actually experiences is the net of the two — and the more turbulent the state, the more of the cut is swallowed. Rate policy works across the cycle; the argument predicts it works least well, relative to the bar the firm is actually facing, at the bottom of it.

And QE is defined by the state it is used in. Martin and Milas make this point in their first paragraph, more flatly than Bowdler and Radia do:

> [QE programmes] are only undertaken in times of severe economic difficulty and in circumstances when more conventional monetary policy cannot be used because the policy rate is at the zero lower bound.
>
> — *Martin & Milas, p. 750*

> **QE is not a weaker version of a rate cut. It is a rate cut administered in the one state that blunts rate cuts.** The zero lower bound is reached in a crisis, and a crisis is exactly the environment in which the option to wait is most valuable. That is a claim about states, and section 8 concedes that on this evidence it cannot be separated from a claim about size.

### The hardest version of the objection

There is a better form of the same challenge, and it nearly kills the argument. Uncertainty and the discount rate are not independent. QE is widely credited with *reducing* uncertainty — calming markets, signalling commitment, stopping a panic. And in the real-options framework, falling uncertainty lowers the investment trigger and releases the projects that were being held back. So on my own model, QE should be precisely the thing that starts a recovery.

My answer turns on a distinction between two kinds of uncertainty, and Dixit supplies the tool for making it. Not all of a distribution matters for an investment decision. Citing Bernanke, he states the restriction:

> Bernanke (1983) found the answer and called it the *bad news principle*: "of possible future outcomes, only the unfavorable ones have a bearing on the current propensity to undertake a given project." In other words, the downside risk is the primary force governing optimal investment decisions when waiting is possible.
>
> — *Dixit, p. 118*

The very next sentence qualifies it, and since the qualification cuts against me I had better quote that too: "To be more precise, the total upside probability matters, but not the shape of the distribution of revenues to the right of the optimal trigger" (p. 118). So it is not that only the bad half of the distribution counts. The chance of ending up above the trigger counts as well — and that is a quantity a policy which supports demand can move.

Even so, the principle is a sharper statement than "uncertainty matters", and it does most of the work I need. What QE compresses is the *price of risk in financial markets* — spreads, volatility, the premium demanded for holding illiquid or long-dated assets. But the downside that governs an irreversible commitment is a different object: it is the lower tail of the firm's *own revenue* distribution — whether customers will exist in three years, whether the currency union will hold, whether the firm's sector will still be there.

I have to be careful how strongly I put this, because section 10 concedes that the wealth and consumption channels stand, and a firm's revenue is other people's spending. If QE raises household spending at all, it thins the firm's bad tail somewhat, since the worst states for a firm are demand-collapse states. So the honest claim is one of degree rather than kind: what a central bank reaches through asset prices is a modest, indirect improvement in the demand outlook, set against doubts — sovereign solvency, the survival of a currency union, whether a whole sector has a future — that I take to have loomed large over the period, though I have no measurement of that and section 9 records it as unestablished. Nor can I say a bond purchase does not touch those, because buying sovereign bonds is quite obviously an instrument aimed at sovereign solvency, and later programmes were designed as exactly that. What I would say is that what a central bank reaches directly is the price at which risk trades, and what it reaches at one or two removes is the doubt itself. A term premium can be compressed; a doubt about whether a currency union survives can at best be leaned against.

Dixit's own policy corollary makes the contrast concrete, because he says exactly what an instrument would have to do to move entry:

> If the aim is to induce quicker new investment or entry of firms, it is especially important to reduce the downside risk.
>
> — *Dixit, p. 123*

Dixit's own worked example of that corollary is worth following, because it does one thing for me and one against. He asks why Japanese firms of the period invested aggressively and clung on through losses where American firms did neither, and answers that the Japanese downside was cushioned — by government support and by tolerated cartels in recessions (pp. 122–3). Two consequences follow. Firms with a protected downside enter early, because waiting protects you only from bad news and there is less bad news to be protected from. And by the mirror image, which Dixit calls the good news principle, they exit late, because the value of staying alive is governed by the upside.

The first half supports me: the instrument that moves entry, on this account, is a guarantee against the firm's own bad states — an industrial or fiscal instrument, not a rate. The second half does not. If the decision to stay is governed by the upside, then a policy that props up asset prices and signals that the authorities will act *is* pointed at the right half of the distribution for retarding exit. That is a mechanism, taken straight from my own source, for the half of the Martin and Milas result I was going to explain by market functioning — and it weakens the exit limb I called my strongest, because it means QE plausibly does reach that margin.

With that conceded, it is still worth noticing where the state's guarantees in this period actually pointed. The nearest thing to a downside-truncating instrument in Bowdler and Radia's own survey is the National Loan Guarantee Scheme, under which "investors acquiring bank debt up to this limit face the same level of risk as they would do purchasing gilts" (pp. 618–19). The guarantee removed a downside — for the people lending to the banks. Nothing in it removed any downside facing the firm deciding whether to build. I should be honest that this observation does less for me than it first appears, because the scheme was aimed at the cost of borrowing for small and medium-sized enterprises, and section 10 scopes those firms out of my claim entirely. It illustrates the shape of the problem; it is not evidence for the part of the argument that survives.

There is supporting evidence in Martin and Milas, and it is the passage I found most useful in the whole survey:

> As in the US, the strongest movements in UK government bond rates occurred outside periods of QE programmes. In particular, the periods leading up to bail-outs for Greece (May 2010 and July 2011…), Ireland (November 2010), and Portugal (May 2011) are associated with falls in UK and US government bond rates, **clearly suggestive of a 'flight-to-quality' around these events, irrespective of the effects of QE**.
>
> — *Martin & Milas, p. 754*

One thing follows from this and one does not, and I had better separate them. What does not follow is that the eurozone shocks were the *kind* of uncertainty QE cannot reach. A sovereign bail-out is itself a financial-market event, and its effect here is measured in a financial price; the passage shows the shocks were large, not that they were of a different species. I had originally written that inference down and it does not hold.

What does follow is more modest and more useful. Those episodes are a rough natural experiment: borrowing costs for the safest borrowers fell sharply for reasons owing nothing to monetary policy, and no investment boom followed. Even that has a real weakness. A flight to quality is precisely the moment when safe and risky rates part company — gilts rally while corporate spreads widen — so the fall in the risk-free rate may not have reached the firms in question at all. But that is, in its own way, another instance of the same problem: the risk-free leg of Bowdler and Radia's channel failing to arrive where the decision gets made.

## 7. The second friction: the hurdle rate as a queue

Everything so far has been a friction in the firm's *environment* — irreversibility is a property of the capital, uncertainty a property of the world. There is a second friction, and it sits inside the organisation itself. It is the one that makes this an argument in management as well as economics.

> **SOURCE — THE MANAGEMENT EVIDENCE**
> Steven Sharpe and Gustavo Suarez, *'Why Isn't Investment More Sensitive to Interest Rates? Evidence from Surveys'*
> Federal Reserve Board, FEDS 2014-02 (revised 2015); published in *Management Science*. A study of what chief financial officers say they would actually do, drawn from the Duke/CFO Magazine survey.

The title is the argument, and the finding is the one that sent me looking. Asked directly what they would do if borrowing costs fell, most finance directors said their investment plans would not change. I will withdraw that as evidence later in this section — it measures the wrong thing for the claim I end up making — but it is what pointed me at the practice behind the answer.

That practice is the **hurdle rate**: a minimum return a project must promise before it is funded, written into a capital policy, applied by a board to proposals it cannot itself verify. It sits far above the firm's cost of capital, and it is reported at similar heights in surveys taken decades apart, in different countries, across a period in which long-term government yields collapsed. Those surveys are not a series and I will not treat them as one. But something is holding that number up.

> **Corroborating work.** Jagannathan, Matsa, Meier and Tarhan, *'Why do firms use high discount rates?'*, *Journal of Financial Economics* 120(3), 2016. And the Reserve Bank of Australia's *Bulletin*, December 2021, *'Why Are Investment Hurdle Rates So Sticky?'*, which reports a median hurdle around 13 per cent against a representative cost of capital nearer 6 per cent, and firms explicitly declining to lower it as rates fell. I have this at second hand and should not be more specific about the comparator than my source is.

### ▸ What I thought reading it

I expected the answer to be dramatic and it was mundane, which is what convinced me. There is no crisis mechanism here, no market failure, no panic. There is a number written down in a capital policy that nobody revised.

Economics files that gap as a friction — a wedge to be measured and then assumed away. But a wedge that persists for decades across thousands of firms is not noise. It is doing a job. And once you ask what job, it stops looking like a friction and starts looking like a governance device.

### The theoretical objection, and why it is the right one

The obvious rebuttal is that a hurdle rate can be a governance device *and* still fall with the cost of capital — keep the premium constant, lower the base, and the screen still screens while policy still transmits. Worse for me, the rebuttal has a distinguished author. Dixit's explicit purpose in his section on option values is to explain observed hurdle rates: "Summers' (1987) finding of median hurdle rates of 15 percent is no longer a puzzle" (p. 117). If he is right, section 5 has already accounted for the hurdle rate and section 7 is redundant.

**So let me concede the size of the gap outright.** Dixit's mechanism produces hurdle rates in the low-to-mid teens from a cost of capital in the low single figures, on volatilities he treats as ordinary. That is the observed range. Any argument of mine that rested on the *height* of the hurdle rate would be arguing against a theory that already explains it, and would lose.

The disagreement is about what kind of object the number is, and that is a theoretical question rather than a measurement one.

In Dixit's account the hurdle rate is *derived from* the cost of capital, the volatility of the market the firm sells into and, in his footnote, the trend growth of its revenue. All three are outside the firm as an organisation, and a change in any of them changes the hurdle rate. It is an external price wearing a disguise.

The claim I want to make is that in practice it is anchored to something inside. And the reason is nothing to do with finance.

How much of the observed number that internal anchor accounts for, I cannot say. Dixit's channel and mine are not mutually exclusive; the question is which one the number moves with.

### The hurdle rate as a rationing device

Start with an assumption, stated as one because it is doing a lot of work and because it can be wrong. In a large firm the binding constraint on capital projects is not money. It is management. If money is what binds, then the marginal project's opportunity cost is a financing cost, it moves with the cost of finance, and this section collapses back into section 5. I take the assumption from the fact that the firms this argument is scoped to — large, capital-market-access firms — are the ones least likely to be short of external funds, and from the ordinary observation that boards run out of people to run things before they run out of money. But it is an assumption, and it is the first place I would look for the flaw.

Grant it, and here is the picture. In any firm of size, more projects are proposed than can be funded and, more importantly, than can be *managed*. Divisional managers submit optimistic forecasts, because advocating for your division is what a divisional manager is for, and the board cannot verify the forecasts it receives. Its problem is therefore not valuation. It is triage: which of these do we actually have the money and the attention to run properly?

A threshold set to solve that problem is anchored to the opportunity cost of *the next project in the queue* — the best thing the firm would have to drop in order to take this one. That is an internal quantity. It is set by how many good ideas the organisation is generating and how much management it has to run them with. And neither of those changes when gilt yields fall.

Two consequences follow, and they are the reason this is a different claim rather than the same one restated.

First, a rationing threshold has no reason to track the cost of capital, because the cost of capital is not the quantity it is rationing. A firm with more projects than managers is short of managers, and cheaper money does not lengthen anybody's working week.

Second, a threshold that *did* move every time the risk-free rate moved would stop doing its job. Its usefulness as a screen depends on being a stable commitment that divisions can plan against and cannot argue with. A number that floats is a number to be lobbied about, and the whole point of the device is to end the lobbying.

These two are not the same argument and I should not let them blur, because they have different implications. The first is about what the number is anchored to and predicts that it does not follow the cost of capital at all. The second is about commitment and predicts something weaker: that whatever the number is anchored to, it will be revised rarely and in steps. The second would explain sticky hurdle rates even if the threshold were the cost of capital marked up exactly as Dixit says — it would just be a marked-up cost of capital that nobody had got round to recomputing. So the commitment argument is a third explanation rather than a second leg of mine, and the evidence I go on to cite fits it at least as well as it fits the queue.

There is also a question about the anchor itself that I cannot fully answer. Whether a project counts as "good enough for the queue" depends on how the queue is ranked, and if it is ranked by net present value then a fall in the discount rate lifts every project's value and reshuffles the order, so the marginal project is not invariant to the cost of capital after all. The invariance I am claiming holds if the threshold is applied to a pipeline of ideas whose supply is set by the organisation rather than by the discount rate. That is what I believe happens, and it is not something I can show.

One step remains, and it is the step the whole paper is for, so it should be spelled out rather than assumed. A hurdle rate is not a number in a report. It is the gate a proposal has to pass to enter the capital budget at all. If the gate moves on the organisation's clock rather than on the central bank's, then a fall in the cost of finance does not change the set of proposals admitted this year, or next year, and changes it only as the pipeline and the management stock change. A project not admitted is not built.

I should say what that does and does not deliver, since the earlier version of this paragraph claimed more. It does not give an absent quantity in the national accounts. It gives a lagged one: investment that arrives on the firm's schedule instead of the policy's, which for an instrument meant to work within a cycle is close enough to failure to matter, and is not the same thing as never. That is the honest shape of the claim, and it is weaker than the one I started with.

> **In Dixit's account the hurdle rate is the cost of capital marked up. In this account it is closer to the shadow price of managerial attention.** Two numbers that look identical on a slide and have different things behind them.

That last comparison hides a real problem and I would rather open it than leave it. A shadow price on a capacity constraint is naturally a value per unit of the scarce input — per manager-year, say — and a hurdle rate is a return per pound of capital per year. Those are not the same units, and they coincide only if the management a project absorbs runs roughly with the capital it absorbs, which is not generally true: a large simple asset purchase can need less attention than a small fiddly reorganisation. So a percentage threshold on capital is a crude instrument for rationing attention.

I think that is a point in favour of the reading rather than against it. A firm optimising properly against a management constraint would not use a single percentage threshold at all. That firms do use one is what you would expect of a *governance* device — something whose value lies in being simple, uniform, hard to argue with and applicable by people who cannot verify the forecasts in front of them. The crudeness is the feature. But I am aware that this is a story about why an instrument is crude, and a critic could reasonably read the same fact as evidence that the attention constraint is not what is being rationed.

### What separates them, and what the evidence can say

The two accounts make different predictions, and the difference is a slope rather than a level.

Dixit's own two calibrations say how steep his slope is, and reading them off is worth doing, though the answer comes with a trap in it. At a 5 per cent cost of capital his mark-up is 1.86, giving a hurdle rate of 9.3 per cent. At 2 per cent the mark-up is 2.61. Multiplying gives 5.2 per cent — that product is my arithmetic, not a figure he prints.

Now the trap, which I would rather set out than fall into. Measured in percentage points, a three-point fall in the cost of capital produces a four-point fall in the hurdle rate: it moves *more* than the cost of capital does. Measured proportionally, the same two numbers say the opposite — the cost of capital falls by three-fifths and the hurdle rate by not much over two-fifths, which is the reading section 5 used. Both are correct. Neither is the "true" one, and any argument that needs a particular one of them has to say why.

So the strongest form of the claim available to me is the form that does not depend on the choice: **in Dixit's model the hurdle rate moves substantially when the cost of capital moves, on any measure.** It cannot be indifferent to it.

One qualification, which is his and not mine. That slope holds with the volatility of revenue held fixed. His mark-up depends on volatility too, so a hurdle rate could sit still while the cost of capital fell if uncertainty rose enough to offset it — which is a rival I return to at the end of this section, and which section 6 gives some reason to think was true of the crisis years.

The rationing account predicts something different in kind. Its threshold is tied to the pipeline of proposals and the stock of management, so it changes when those change — over years, on the organisation's own clock — and not when gilt yields change. The strongest form of that, a threshold which never moves at all, is not one I can defend: objection 4 explains why. The defensible form is that its clock is the wrong clock for monetary policy.

I have to be careful about what my own sources can say to that.

Sharpe and Suarez do not settle it, and I had been treating them as though they did. They ask whether *investment plans* would change if borrowing costs fell. A firm sitting well below its trigger can honestly answer no while its hurdle rate is falling, so their finding is consistent with Dixit rather than a refutation of him. Jagannathan and co-authors establish that the wedge exists, not that it is immobile. Only the Reserve Bank's study is on the right variable, reporting firms explicitly declining to lower their thresholds as rates fell — and I have that at second hand.

There is one study that has effectively run the test. Gormsen and Huber report that firms' discount rates track the cost of capital far less than one for one, and adjust only over many years. I have this from a secondary survey and have not read it, so I offer it as the state of play rather than as my evidence.

It is tempting to set "far less than one for one" against the slope just read off Dixit and declare a win, and I nearly did. I should not, for two reasons. I do not know from a secondary summary what "one for one" is measured in, and the paragraph above shows that the answer flips with the measure — so the comparison rests on a commensurability I cannot check. And movement that is partial *and lagged by years* is exactly what the commitment explanation predicts: a threshold that is a marked-up cost of capital all right, but one nobody has got round to recomputing. That is the third explanation I separated out two paragraphs ago, and this evidence fits it at least as well as it fits the queue.

The fair statement is that the one study on the right variable finds movement, which is against a threshold that never moves, and finds it small and slow, which is against a threshold recomputed continuously. It sits between us, and I would be reading it tendentiously to say otherwise.

I should also record that my own sources prefer the rival. The Reserve Bank's Bulletin gives the value of waiting under irreversibility as one of its explanations for sticky hurdle rates; Sharpe and Suarez lean towards financial slack, which is a third mechanism I have not confronted at all. I am disagreeing with the people I am citing, which is allowed, but it should be visible.

### A last honest word on scope

Section 6 argues that firm-level uncertainty is high in exactly the states where rates get cut. Someone could use that against me here: if uncertainty rose while the cost of capital fell, Dixit's mark-up would widen just as its base narrowed, and the hurdle rate would sit still for reasons that have nothing to do with queues. That is a real rival and I cannot exclude it for the crisis years, which are unfortunately the years QE was running. What it does not obviously explain is why hurdle rates in the low-to-mid teens have been reported from calm periods too, decades apart, in different countries, by different surveys. But those surveys are not a series, and I would not pretend otherwise.

## 8. What this does to Martin and Milas

> **PRIMARY SOURCE — THE EVIDENCE**
> Christopher Martin and Costas Milas, *'Quantitative easing: a sceptical survey'*
> Oxford Review of Economic Policy, 28(4), 2012, pp. 750–764 — the same issue as Bowdler and Radia, who discuss it at pp. 605 and 611 of their own article. Read on the same programme.

Martin and Milas survey the empirical work and reach a two-part conclusion that I had originally read as a single verdict:

> QE increased GDP growth by around 1–3 per cent with a similar effect on inflation. This evidence suggests that **QE has proved effective in limiting the scale of the downturn** caused by the financial crisis that began in 2007. However, it suggests that **QE, by itself, is not strong enough to spark an economic recovery**. Of course, this conclusion is tentative.
>
> — *Martin & Milas, p. 762*

### ▸ What I thought reading it — the second time

The first time through this was a result: here is what the econometrics says, note it down. What changed after Dixit was that the asymmetry stopped looking like a finding and started looking like something that needed explaining. Why would a policy be good at preventing a collapse and bad at starting a recovery? Those are not obviously different tasks.

It was uncomfortable to realise I had read the sentence and never asked whether there was a reason for it.

---

### First, the answer that needs no mechanism at all

Before offering one, I should deal with the possibility that there is nothing here to explain. Martin and Milas supply their own reading on the next page, and it is a claim about size rather than about states:

> However, QE is a rather weak policy instrument; the very large initial asset-purchase programmes had effects comparable to a reduction of 200–300 bp in the policy rate; subsequent programmes had little effect.
>
> — *Martin & Milas, p. 763*

That is a complete alternative explanation. A dose worth two or three hundred basis points might well be enough to cushion a fall and not enough to reverse one, and no state-dependence is needed to say so. I cannot rule it out, and I should not pretend the puzzle is airtight when the authors themselves have supplied a deflationary reading of it.

What my account adds is a difference in principle. If the asymmetry is about dose, the same dose should work about as well whenever it is administered. If it is about state, the same dose should work better in calm conditions than in a crisis.

The trouble is that this difference cannot be tested on QE, and section 6 is the reason: quantitative easing is only ever administered at the zero lower bound, so the calm-conditions observation does not exist. It could be tested on conventional rate cuts, at which point it is no longer a claim about QE. So the two readings are rivals in principle and, on this evidence, not separable in practice. I would rather say that than manufacture a puzzle and solve it.

### The mechanism, if one is wanted

The reason my argument supplies is that the two tasks load on the same state variable in opposite directions.

| Task | What it needs | Strongest when |
|---|---|---|
| **Preventing collapse** | Markets that clear, a floor under asset prices, and — by the good news principle — an upside propped up, since the value of staying alive is governed by the upside | Uncertainty is high: there is dysfunction to repair, and a great deal of upside to restore |
| **Starting a recovery** | Firms making irreversible commitments of capital, which the bad news principle says turns mainly on the downside | Uncertainty is low — the option to wait is cheap |

That table is the sharpest form of the argument, and section 6 supplies the reason the first row works better than the second. Dixit's good news principle says the decision to *stay* is governed by the possible good outcomes. A policy that lifts asset prices and signals commitment is pointed at exactly that half of the distribution, so it should retard exit — which is what preventing a collapse largely consists of. For entry the same policy is pointed at the less useful half, though section 6 concedes that "the less useful half" is the right phrase and "the wrong half" is not: Dixit's own qualification is that the total probability of ending above the trigger counts too, and a policy that supports demand can move that. Two of the claims in that table are mine rather than his — that the upside-propping works best when uncertainty is high, and that the two rows therefore pull apart — and neither follows from anything Dixit prints.

QE is deployed in the state where the first is easy and the second is hardest. It stabilises without stimulating. That is a mechanism for the pattern Martin and Milas report, and one they do not offer — though, as the previous subsection concedes, they do offer a magnitude account of it, and mine is a rival to theirs rather than a filling of a vacuum.

Dixit's zone of inaction supplies a second and more specific reason, and it explains the *asymmetry in time* rather than the asymmetry in task. Here it matters to be exact about what the instrument does, because it is easy to picture it backwards. Cheaper finance works mainly by lowering the trigger towards the firm's revenue, not by lifting the firm's revenue towards the trigger. (Section 10 concedes it does a little of the second too, through the demand that higher asset prices support; that is a real channel and not one I am disputing.) So the question is how far the trigger moves relative to how far away it was.

Dixit's own base case shows the shape of the answer. With a running cost of 1 and a sunk cost of 2, the exit trigger sits at 0.72, Marshall's entry level at 1.10, and the true entry trigger at 1.62. A firm at revenue 1 sits exactly at Marshall's shutdown point — it covers its running costs and earns nothing above them — and it is six-tenths of a unit short of the level at which Dixit says it should build. Most of that distance is the mark-up for the option to wait: Marshall's own bar accounts for a sixth of the gap, and the other five-sixths is what waiting is worth.

A cut in the cost of capital reaches that gap by two routes and they pull against each other. It lowers the interest on the sunk cost, which in this calibration is a tenth of the entry level, the running cost carrying the other nine-tenths and caring nothing about gilt yields. And it widens the mark-up, for the reason section 5 gave. How the two net out I cannot say: Dixit states plainly that once abandonment is allowed the two triggers are fixed by a system with no closed-form solution, and the second method note in section 13 records that I will not estimate what he declines to compute.

I should be honest that this illustration does not generalise in my favour, and possibly not at all. The nine-tenths is a property of Dixit's chosen numbers — "suppose that K = 2", he writes, a sunk cost worth two years of running costs, which is a light-capital activity. Raise the capital intensity towards a chemical plant and the interest on the sunk stake becomes a much larger share of the entry level, so the cut has more to bite on. Section 10 scopes this argument to exactly that kind of investment. And the split of that gap — a sixth of it accounted for by Marshall's bar, five-sixths by the mark-up above it — is calibration-dependent in the same way, so I cannot claim the proportion holds anywhere but here. Both fractions are mine, worked from his figures.

What survives the calibration is not a proportion but a structure: there are two bars, the second sits above the first, and the distance between them is put there by uncertainty and by the discount rate together — and the second of those, as section 5 showed, moves the wrong way. A cheaper cost of finance is only obliquely aimed at that distance. The worked numbers show what such a structure can look like. They do not show how large it is in any real firm, and I would rather lose the demonstration than keep it on false terms.

> **That is the last mile. Not that the instrument does nothing, but that between a firm and its decision stands a second bar, put there by what the firm does not know and by the discount rate together, at which a cheaper cost of finance is only obliquely aimed — and on the second of which it pushes the wrong way.**

And for the firms that crossed the exit trigger on the way down, restoring conditions does not bring them back, because reversing an exit means paying the sunk cost over again. That is hysteresis in Dixit's exact sense: the cause reverses, and the effect does not. I should carry forward the concession section 6 made, though: if QE props up the upside, it holds some firms above the exit trigger who would otherwise have crossed it, so the population this applies to is smaller than I first supposed. What the policy stops from happening does not need explaining. What needs explaining is why, for the firms it did not reach in time, nothing came back.

### Does this survive going from one firm to a whole economy?

It is a fair worry that a single-firm option calculation says nothing about aggregate investment, which is what QE is aimed at. Dixit's section on competitive industry dynamics speaks to it. Put many option-holding firms together, let entry cap the price at the high trigger and exit floor it at the low one, and the industry's equilibrium triggers turn out to be the same two a lone firm would choose — a result he attributes to Leahy, and one he states for the case where demand follows the particular random process he has been assuming (p. 126).

I cannot take that transfer for granted, and it is worth saying why. The result is about a competitive industry of identical atomistic price-takers with free entry, and Dixit adds that in such an industry each firm's pure waiting value is competed away to nothing. The firms this paper is scoped to — large, with capital-market access and written capital policies — are not that population. So the aggregation result tells me the mechanism survives being embedded in a market, which is what I needed to know; it does not license carrying my own scoped claim over to the market unchanged. His conclusion is worth stating in full, because it is a statement about what a normal, competitive, well-functioning industry looks like:

> Therefore we are likely to see significant periods of supernormal profits with no new entry, and of operating losses without exit, in the course of a competitive industry's equilibrium evolution.
>
> — *Dixit, p. 126*

That sentence is the aggregate version of the whole argument. An economy sitting inside its band is an economy that looks frozen — profitable firms not expanding, loss-making firms not closing — and none of that stillness is evidence of anything being broken, or of any policy having failed to arrive. It is what a competitive economy full of option-holders does.

### Their own explanation, and why I am not replacing it

To be fair to them, they do offer an account of a related puzzle — why later rounds of QE did so much less than the first:

> This may be because they were introduced when bond rates were already quite low. Or it may be because initial QE programmes were sufficient to demonstrate the commitment of policy-makers to addressing the financial crisis, leaving little for subsequent programmes to add.
>
> — *Martin & Milas, pp. 762–3*

Both are plausible and neither competes with my argument, because they explain a different failure. Theirs accounts for the **price leg failing across successive rounds**: each new programme moved yields less than the last. Mine accounts for the **spending leg failing across states**: why the firms that *did* see cheaper finance still did not build. Both can hold at once. If anything they compound — a smaller price movement feeding into a less responsive decision.

### The scoping constraint their evidence imposes on me

Martin and Milas also contain a finding that constrains what I can claim, and I would rather state it myself than have it produced against me:

> …we note that QE does not appear to have affected interest rates facing small and medium enterprises and households, especially rates on unsecured borrowing. UK evidence… suggests that effective interest rates on this type of borrowing increased in 2008–9 as widening spreads outweighed the effects of lower policy rates.
>
> — *Martin & Milas, p. 758*

For SMEs, the price never arrived. Their non-investment is fully explained by Bowdler and Radia's own access qualification and needs nothing from me. So my claim has to be scoped to the firms the cheaper price *did* reach: large firms with capital-market access, which is also where the internal-rationing argument is strongest — not because those firms are short of managerial attention — an owner-manager is shorter still, which is why section 7 does not rest the argument on scarcity of attention alone — but because they are the firms that ration by a written threshold rather than by the owner's judgement, and are least likely to be short of external funds.

The Bank of England's own account says the same thing about bank-dependent borrowers, which is worth noting because it comes from the institution running the policy:

> There is, however, little evidence that effective new bank lending rates for households or firms fell significantly following QE purchases, even though there were substantial falls in the spread between interbank lending rates and OIS rates over the period. But it is hard to estimate what the counterfactual would have been.
>
> — *Joyce, Tong & Woods, Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin 2011 Q3, p. 208*

### One further caution about the evidence base

Martin and Milas warn in their abstract that the literature is *"limited, relying on similar methodologies and largely originating in central banks."* That warning is doing real work: the 1–3 per cent figure I am treating as a fact to be explained comes largely from central bank research programmes, and the paper flags its own conclusion as tentative. There is later work suggesting the concern was well founded — Fabo and co-authors' *'Fifty Shades of QE'* reports that central bank papers find larger QE effects than academic ones. I take that from a secondary survey and have not read it directly, so I would offer it as a line of inquiry rather than a finding.

## 9. Objections, and how far I can answer them

*These are the attacks the argument has to survive. On some I think I have an answer; on others I have a direction and not much more, and I would rather mark which is which.*

---

**OBJECTION 1** — *Bowdler and Radia's exception is about governments. Dixit's irreversibility is about firms. Why should a firm-level theory dissolve a government-level exception?*

**Answered.** It does not, and I am not claiming it does. The exception is not evidence for my mechanism; it is the thing that prompted me to look for one. What the two cases share is only structural: a feature of the decision itself — its horizon in their case, its irreversibility in mine — rather than of the price faced, severing the link between them. And as section 4 concedes, their sentence is most likely orthodox optimising economics rather than anything organisational, which makes it a prompt and not a warrant. Bowdler and Radia allow that structure once and never test whether it recurs. My claim is that it does, for an entirely different reason.

---

**OBJECTION 2** — *You say QE compresses only financial uncertainty. But that is asserted. Your evidence — big bond moves around the eurozone bail-outs — is a fact about a financial price.*

**Half-answered, and this is the weakest joint in the paper.** Dixit's bad news principle tells me *which part* of the distribution has to move for investment to respond — mainly the lower tail of the firm's own revenue prospects — and that is a real gain in precision over "uncertainty matters". But the claim that QE reaches that tail only weakly is a theoretical claim I find plausible and have not established. The bail-out episodes are consistent with it and do not demonstrate it.

I should also correct the test I originally proposed, because section 6 has moved and it did not move with it. I first said the demonstration would be a measure of firm-level demand uncertainty that *fails to fall* around QE announcements while financial volatility does. That tests a claim I no longer make. Section 6 now concedes that the demand channel thins the firm's bad tail somewhat, that the total upside probability counts too, and that sovereign purchases are pointed at sovereign doubt — so my claim is one of degree, and a null result would refute it rather than confirm it. The right test is a differential: firm-level demand uncertainty falling by materially less, and materially later, than financial volatility around the same announcements. Survey-based expectations panels are the obvious instrument. What I cannot supply is a threshold for "materially", which is another way of saying the claim is not merely unestablished but unquantified.

---

**OBJECTION 3** — *Real options gives you delay, not destruction. When uncertainty falls the projects come back, possibly in a burst. Have you not just described a lag and called it a failure?*

**Half-answered, and it splits into a half I can partly defend and a half I cannot.** The half I can partly defend is exit — partly, because section 6 concedes that by the good news principle QE plausibly does reach the exit margin and holds some firms in place, which narrows this to the firms that crossed anyway. For a firm that crossed L and quit, coming back means paying the sunk cost again, so a return of conditions to the pre-crisis level does not restore the project: "a subsequent rise back to 1 in revenues will not restore the project; there is hysteresis in the reverse direction, too" (p. 122). That is destruction, not delay. Two honest qualifications. Dixit's own worked case, the 1980s dollar, runs the other direction — it is firms that entered and would not leave, not firms that left and would not return — so it illustrates the shape of hysteresis without evidencing this particular limb. And the limb rests on his assumption that rusting is total and immediate, which he flags as a simplification. Where a firm can mothball rather than scrap, exit is reversible at less than full cost and this half weakens too.

The half I cannot defend is deferral. Inside Dixit's model, waiting is *optimal*: the option retains its value, the firm has lost nothing, and the project fires when conditions warrant. Nothing in the source turns that into a loss. To call it one I would need an argument that the private option value and the social value of the project come apart — that capacity absent when demand returns, or market position taken by a competitor who did build, costs the economy more than the firm's own calculation registers. I believe that, and I have not shown it, and it is not in Dixit. So: the exit case is answered; the deferral case is a claim about magnitudes and externalities that I have not measured. That is the honest grade.

---

**OBJECTION 4** — *Managerial attention is a resource. If the projects are worth doing, why does the firm not simply hire more managers — especially now that finance is cheap?*

**Answered, the answer is borrowed, and it costs me something I did not expect.** Attention is a slowly-adjusting stock rather than a purchasable flow: new managers have to be trained and absorbed by the incumbent ones, so the constraint relaxes over years, not over the horizon on which a rate cut is supposed to act. This is essentially Penrose's argument about the limits on the rate at which a firm can grow. It is a good answer and it is not mine.

The cost is this. If the constraint relaxes over years, then the threshold it sets should also fall over years — partially, and with a long lag. That is not "no movement". It is very close to what Gormsen and Huber report, and closer still to the commitment explanation I conceded in section 7 as a third rival. So the best available answer to this objection weakens the sharpest form of my own prediction. The version I can still defend is that the threshold moves on the organisation's clock rather than on the central bank's, which is a weaker claim and, I think, the true one.

---

**OBJECTION 5** — *Is the hurdle-rate premium not simply the option value of waiting under a different name? In which case sections 5 and 7 are one argument. Dixit himself says his mechanism dissolves the hurdle-rate puzzle.*

**Answered, but with the size of the gap conceded.** Dixit is right that his mechanism produces hurdle rates of the observed height from a cost of capital of the observed height, and I say so in section 7. Nothing in my argument turns on the number being large.

The two accounts differ in what the number is anchored to. Dixit's hurdle rate is derived from three things outside the firm as an organisation — the cost of capital, the volatility of its market and the trend growth of its revenue — and, holding the other two fixed, his own calibrations have it moving substantially with the first — section 7 shows that whether "substantially" reads as more or less than one for one depends on the measure, and declines to pick one. A rationing threshold is anchored to the opportunity cost of the next project in an internal queue, which is set by how many good ideas the firm has and how much management it has to run them. The sharpest version of that — a threshold that never moves — is not one I can defend, for the reasons objection 4 gives. What I would defend is that such a threshold moves on the organisation's clock rather than on the central bank's: it changes when the pipeline or the management stock changes, over years, not when gilt yields change. That is a difference in what the number is, and it makes a difference to the slope that could be looked for.

What I should not claim is that the contest has been settled in my favour. The best estimate I know of — movement that is partial and lagged by years — sits between the two predictions, and fits a third explanation better than either: a marked-up cost of capital that nobody recomputes often. And the two mechanisms are correlated in any real firm, since a board may well set a high threshold precisely *because* the capital is irreversible.

---

**OBJECTION 6** — *Bowdler and Radia's sentence says cheaper finance boosts consumption* and *investment. You have said nothing about consumption. How much of the theory have you actually damaged?*

**Conceded.** Not much of it, if damage means refutation. See section 10.

---

**OBJECTION 7** — *Dixit himself names the case that destroys your mechanism. If several firms can seize the same opportunity, "waiting is no longer feasible" and the Marshallian trigger is valid (p. 118). Most real investment is contested. Have you not lost the argument on the author's own terms?*

**Answered, and again by the source, though less completely than I first thought.** The exception is real but narrow: it requires an opportunity that a rival can take away entirely — one site, one licence, one first-mover position. Dixit's own treatment of competitive industries, where many identical firms can all build, reaches the opposite conclusion: the triggers survive, because the price is capped by entry and floored by exit, and the equilibrium triggers are the single firm's (p. 126). Section 8 records the limit on that answer, and I should repeat it here rather than bank the result twice: the industry Dixit is describing is one of identical atomistic price-takers whose pure waiting value is competed away to nothing, which is not the population this paper is scoped to. So for the firms Dixit models, competition as such does not restore the Marshallian rule; only a genuinely scarce and seizable prize does. What competition does to a large firm with market power is a question neither of us has answered. I should add that the same section names two further forces that push towards early action — first-mover commitment value, and firms inferring each other's information — and I have not weighed either.

---

**OBJECTION 8** — *Your own hinge quotation supplies a simpler explanation than either of your frictions. If governments ignore "cyclical" rate movements because they are temporary, then firms may have ignored QE for exactly the same reason: they read it as transitory. That is a pure cost-of-capital story, inside Bowdler and Radia's own framework, needing nothing from Dixit and nothing from management.*

**Not answered, and it is the strongest objection in this list.** It fits the evidence at least as well as mine. It explains why later rounds did less, since a policy repeatedly announced and repeatedly wound down looks more temporary each time. It fits Martin and Milas's finding that the effects on bond rates unwound. It is consistent with everything in section 8 and needs no organisational premise at all.

Three things I would say, none of them a refutation. First, the two are not exclusive; a permanent-looking cut into an economy where the gate is an internal threshold still does not get through. Second, transitoriness is a reason not to *value* the cheaper finance highly, whereas my argument is about the decision procedure being insensitive to how the finance is valued — different mechanisms with different fingerprints, since a transitory-perceptions story predicts investment responds fully to changes read as permanent. Third, real-options theory actually sharpens the transitory story rather than competing with it: a cut believed to be temporary is exactly the kind of information that makes waiting more attractive, since the option to wait is most valuable when the current state may not last. That is Dixit's own reasoning applied to the policy rather than to demand.

Which of the two does more of the work is an empirical question that this paper does not settle and should not pretend to.

## 10. Scope: what this argument does not claim

Overclaiming would be the easiest way to lose this argument, so it is worth being explicit about the boundaries.

- **It is not a claim that QE does not work.** The evidence that asset purchases moved bond and corporate yields is the most robust part of the literature. I have not disputed it and do not need to.

- **It leaves the wealth channel standing.** Higher asset prices do make asset-holders wealthier, and some of that is spent. That is a separate leg of Bowdler and Radia's section III(ii) and nothing here touches it — though its reach is uneven: De Luigi and co-authors find that a third of euro-area households hold neither housing nor risky financial assets and so sit outside the asset-price channel altogether. That leaves two-thirds inside it, which is the more important side of the finding and not one I should quietly drop.

- **It leaves consumption standing.** Households refinancing at lower rates have more disposable income, and intertemporal substitution is real. My argument is about capital formation, and my reason for caring about capital formation specifically is that a recovery in capacity is what a recovery in output eventually requires.

- **It is not a claim that the theory is false.** It is a claim about *completeness*. The chain is modelled with frictions at every stage except the last, and the last stage is where a quantity finally has to change.

- **It is scoped to firms with capital-market access.** For bank-dependent SMEs the price never fell, and the existing access qualification explains their behaviour without any of this.

- **It is scoped to irreversible capital formation that is not a race.** Where the opportunity can be seized by a rival, Dixit's own qualification applies and the textbook rule returns.

> The strongest honest version: **Bowdler and Radia's account is rigorous about frictions in markets and frictionless about frictions in organisations.** That is not a flaw in their reasoning. It is a boundary of the discipline they are writing in — and the discipline on the other side of it is management.

## 11. A test I could actually run

An argument of this shape is worth little without a way of being wrong. Working out what the tests should be was the most useful thing that reading Dixit closely did for me, because my first instinct was to sort firms by how irreversible their capital is — and that turns out to be the right test for one half of his argument and the wrong test for the other.

**Where the obvious test does and does not have power.** In the simple half of Dixit's model, the one with no exit decision, the mark-up over the textbook trigger depends on uncertainty and on the discount rate, and the *size* of the sunk cost does not enter it. Sorting sectors by how much capital they sink would tell you which has a high bar, not which is least moved when finance gets cheaper. That was my original design, and against the entry margin it has little power.

But it has power against the other margin, and Dixit says so on the same page I take design two from: "An increase in the sunk cost K will obviously raise the investment trigger H. But it will also lower the abandonment trigger L; the project will be continued through periods of greater losses for the option of keeping alive the larger sunk stake" (p. 120). A bigger sunk stake widens the band at both ends. Since the band is what produces hysteresis, and hysteresis is the limb of this argument I can partly defend, sorting by irreversibility is the right test for that limb even though it is the wrong test for entry. "Partly" because section 6 concedes that QE reaches the exit margin through the upside; the design would need to separate firms held in place by the policy from firms that never came near the trigger.

One correction to an earlier draft, since I got this wrong in a way worth recording. I first wrote that the model says the irreversibility test has no power. It does not say that. Dixit's K is sunk by assumption and his model contains no measure of *partial* reversibility — how much you get back on resale — which is what "capital specificity" actually means. On that it is silent rather than hostile.

**Design one: sort by the volatility of revenue.** What the argument does predict is a gradient in uncertainty. Sectors whose revenues swing harder should carry a wider mark-up, and should therefore show less movement in investment for a given proportional change in the cost of finance. It has the right kind of power against the obvious confound: cheap credit and calm conditions arrive together, so any time-series relationship between borrowing costs and investment is hopelessly entangled — but that confound is *aggregate*, hitting every sector in the same period, and an aggregate confound cannot by itself generate a cross-sectional gradient.

**Design two: sort by the cost of exit.** Dixit gives a prediction that is not obvious: "if abandonment is costly—for example, severance payments to workers or the cost of restoring the site of a mine—then the entry trigger is higher; firms are more cautious in undertaking a venture they may have to abandon later at a cost" (p. 120). The two triggers are jointly determined, so anything that makes leaving expensive makes entering rarer. The comparison is of the profitability at which entry actually occurs, across countries with different employment protection or across sectors of different labour intensity within one country. It is a weaker design than the first and I would rather say what it can show than dress it up.

**Design three: measure the movement directly.** Section 7 rests on a claim about a slope: whether a firm's stated hurdle rate moves when its own cost of capital moves. Dixit's account says it must, substantially, holding the volatility and trend growth of revenue fixed. A rationing threshold says it moves on the organisation's clock instead, over years. I had headed this design "the one that separates my two mechanisms", and that is too strong: section 12 concedes I cannot separate the queue from the commitment explanation at all. What this design separates is Dixit's account from the pair of them. Follow a panel of firms over years, with some measure of their own cost of finance, and the question is answerable.

I have to report that this has largely been done, and that I did not know it when I started. Gormsen and Huber find movement far below one for one, arriving over years. That is against a threshold that never moves and against one recomputed continuously, and it is closest of all to the commitment explanation that section 7 concedes is a third rival, so the design as I have described it does not separate the three. Doing that needs a way of telling a threshold that is anchored internally from one that is anchored externally and revised rarely, and I do not have one.

**The things wrong with all this:**

1. **Volatility is correlated with cyclicality.** The sectors with the most volatile revenues are also, broadly, the most demand-sensitive, so an aggregate demand shock might produce a cross-sectional gradient in design one after all. Conditioning on that properly is hard and I am not confident I could do it.

2. **A movement in a trigger is not a movement in investment.** Converting one into the other needs to know how many firms are sitting close to the trigger, which is itself likely to depend on how volatile the sector is. Design one measures the thing my argument is about only indirectly.

3. **Design three needs firms' own cost of capital, which is not observed.** Any proxy — a credit rating, a bond spread, an industry average — introduces measurement error that biases the estimate towards no movement at all, which is unfortunately the direction that flatters me.

4. **Design three has a third parameter loose in it.** Dixit's mark-up depends on the trend growth of revenue as well as on its volatility, and across a panel of real firms and years that varies too. An unmodelled term entering the very quantity being measured is exactly what would confound the slope.

I would rather report designs with named weaknesses than a result I could not defend. And the most useful thing in this section is not any of the three designs. It is that the test I would have run before reading the source turns out to sort firms on a feature that does no work at the margin I thought I was testing, and real work at the margin I had not thought to test. The source did not tell me my instinct was wrong. It told me what my instinct was actually an instinct about.

## 12. Conclusion

Bowdler and Radia set out the transmission mechanism of quantitative easing with real care about where it can break. They name segmentation, limited arbitrage, risk appetite, regulatory mandates on institutional investors, impaired banks and the exclusion of small firms from capital markets. Every one of those frictions sits between the central bank and the price a firm pays for money.

Once, on page 613, they allow a different kind of exception: an agent who faces the cheaper price and is expected not to respond, because of the horizon it decides over rather than what it can borrow. That is a standard assumption about fiscal behaviour and not, as section 4 concedes, a claim about organisations. What it does show is that a feature of the decision itself is allowed to sever the link — once, for one agent, without anyone asking whether anything else in the way a decision gets made does comparable work.

Two things do.

The first is irreversibility under uncertainty. Dixit's argument is that when part of an investment cannot be recovered, the future is unknown, and the chance to build does not expire, then waiting is worth something — so the level a project must clear is the textbook level marked up, and the mark-up grows with uncertainty. That has two consequences for a policy used at the zero lower bound. The lever is weakest in exactly the state that produces the zero lower bound. And because a lower discount rate makes patience cheaper as well as building cheaper, part of the instrument works against itself.

Its second half matters more than its first for a policy trying to restart something. Between the level at which a firm builds and the level at which it quits lies a wide band in which it does neither, and a band that wide means a cause can reverse completely while its effect does not. That is hysteresis, and it is why restoring a price need not restore the quantity the price was supposed to govern. Section 6 narrows this: by Dixit's good news principle a policy that props up the upside holds some firms above the exit trigger who would otherwise have crossed it, so QE plausibly does reach that margin and the firms this applies to are fewer than I first supposed.

The second is the hurdle rate that actually gates capital inside a firm. Dixit explains why that number is high, and I concede it. What I have put up against him is a claim about what kind of number it is: in his model it is derived from quantities outside the firm — the cost of capital, the volatility of its market, the trend growth of its revenue — where in a real capital-budgeting process it looks more like a rationing device, anchored to the next project competing for scarce management — and therefore moving on the organisation's clock, over years, rather than on the central bank's. Section 7 separates that from a second and different explanation, that any published threshold is sticky because a screen which floats is a screen that gets argued with. I raise that one; I do not claim it as support, because it would do the same work without my mechanism.

And I should finish the thought rather than leave it in section 7, because it is the fairest thing anyone could say against this paper. Once the queue's prediction is stated in the form I can defend — movement on the organisation's clock, over years — I do not know of an observation that would separate it from the commitment explanation. They predict the same thing. What separates them is a story about *why*, and a story is not a prediction. Whether the difference between them is worth having is a question I would rather leave open than answer in my own favour.

I should say plainly how that claim stands at the end of this paper, because section 7 gave a good deal of it away. It is a possibility with a mechanism, not a finding. Its central assumption — that management rather than money is what binds — is stated and not shown. Its two supporting arguments turn out to be different explanations rather than one. The only measurement on the right variable lands between my account and Dixit's. And the hurdle-rate-as-governance-device idea has a literature of its own that I have not read properly, so the framing may be the only part of it that is mine.

A word about how the two might fit together, offered as a conjecture and not as a result, since the second of them is a conjecture already. It is tempting to assume two thresholds simply add, and they cannot: applied to the same decision, only the higher one binds. What I would suggest instead is that they act at different stages. A capital budget is built in two moves — proposals are screened in, and admitted projects are then committed to at some moment and stopped at some other. The queue is a screen; the option value is a timing rule and an exit rule.

That would let both matter without either being redundant. But I have to note two things against it. The separation only works if the screening stage ranks proposals on something the option value does not already contain, and section 7 concedes I cannot show that. And the arrangement cuts against my own conclusion: if the option value has the last word on commitment, then a fall in the cost of capital can still fire projects already sitting in the budget, and the policy reaches the margin after all. I would rather leave that standing than tidy it away.

Taken together they bear on something Martin and Milas report: a policy that limits a downturn without sparking a recovery. They read that as a matter of dose, and they may well be right. The reading I have offered is that the two tasks are different in kind. Preventing a collapse asks markets to keep clearing, which QE is genuinely good at in a crisis. Starting a recovery asks firms to commit capital irreversibly, which is what a crisis makes them least willing to do — and what, for those that stopped altogether, a return to normal conditions is not enough to restart. I cannot separate that reading from theirs on this evidence, and there is a third reading, in objection 8, that I like less and cannot dismiss.

So the claim that survives the whole argument is narrower than the one I set out with, and it is a claim about completeness rather than about truth. Bowdler and Radia's chain is modelled with frictions at every stage except the one where a quantity finally has to change. Two candidate frictions belong there. One of them has a rigorous theory and a body of evidence behind it, and I have mostly been reporting it. The other has a mechanism and very little else, and it is the one I think is right.

What I find most interesting about this is not the conclusion but where it came from. The gap in the argument is not an error of reasoning. It is a boundary between two literatures — a place where a macroeconomic model hands off to a decision it does not model, and where the work on how that decision is actually made sits in a different journal, a different faculty and a different half of the same degree.
