# How the argument was arrived at

*A plain-English account of the author's thinking, in the order it happened. No prior knowledge of economics or management is assumed.*

---

The author began with a single article. It was called 'Unconventional Monetary Policy: The Assessment', and it was written in 2012 by Christopher Bowdler, an economist at Oxford, and Amar Radia, who worked at the Bank of England. Its job was to explain how a policy called quantitative easing is supposed to work.

That policy needs explaining first, because everything after this depends on it. After the financial crisis of 2008, central banks wanted people and businesses to spend and build more. A central bank is the public body that looks after a country's money; the Bank of England is ours. The main tool it uses is called monetary policy, which just means the set of things a central bank does to make borrowing easier or harder across the whole country.

Normally it makes borrowing easier by cutting the interest rate. Interest is the extra money a borrower pays a lender for the use of the money, and the interest rate says how much extra, as a percentage per year. Borrow a hundred pounds at five per cent and you pay back a hundred and five after a year. When the central bank pushes that rate down, borrowing gets cheaper everywhere, and the hope is that more people borrow and spend.

By 2009 that tool had run out. The rate had been cut almost to zero, and you cannot cut much below zero. So central banks tried something else. They created new money and used it to buy government debt — that is, the promises a government has already sold to investors, promising to pay them back with interest.

Here it is worth slowing down, because one small idea does a lot of work later. When you buy a piece of government debt, you are buying a promise of fixed future payments. If lots of buyers appear and the price of that promise goes up, you are paying more for the same fixed payments. So the return you get is smaller. **Return** is simply what you get back, as a percentage of what you put in — the lender's side of an interest rate. Price up, return down. That is why buying huge quantities of government debt pushes down the rate of interest on it.

The hope was that this fall would spread outwards. If the safest borrower in the country can borrow more cheaply, everyone else should be able to borrow a bit more cheaply too. And then a company that wanted to build a factory would find the money cheaper than before, and would go ahead and build.

Bowdler and Radia set this out as a chain, and the author found the chain useful precisely because it was a chain. Each link can be examined on its own. The central bank buys the debt. The price of that debt rises and the return on it falls. Other rates follow it down. Borrowing becomes cheaper for ordinary companies. Those companies borrow and build. The country's total production rises. Every step depends on the one before it. If any single link fails, the whole thing stops there.

What the author noticed on his first careful reading was not a mistake. It was a difference in how carefully two neighbouring sentences had been treated. The article says that a fall in these rates *should* boost investment. He kept coming back to that word 'should'.

Look at what the sentence is actually doing. It contains two very different kinds of claim, stitched together. The first is about prices. If the return on government debt falls, the rate an ordinary company pays will probably fall too. That is something you can go and check, and the authors treat it carefully. They give three separate reasons why the cheaper price might never reach the company at all. The banking system was badly damaged at the time, and a damaged bank may not pass anything on. Small companies cannot borrow by selling debt to investors in the first place, so they are cut off from the route the policy travels down. And a bank that is short of money of its own will keep the saving rather than share it. All three are honest, and all three are about the same thing: whether the cheaper price arrives.

The second claim in that sentence is of a completely different kind. It is about what a company actually *does* once the cheaper price has arrived. And that claim is simply stated. No reasons are given why it might fail. No conditions are attached to it.

The author's first reaction was not disagreement. He wants to be clear about that, because it matters for what follows. His first reaction was a kind of itch. Two halves of one sentence were not the same sort of statement at all, and they were being given the same amount of trust. One half was hedged three times. The other was not hedged once.

He read the sentence again, and then read the paragraphs around it, and the feeling changed. It stopped seeming like carelessness. It started seeming like a habit — as though a whole way of working had trained its practitioners to look very hard at markets and not to look at all at the businesses inside them.

He thinks he can name the habit. In this kind of economics a company is treated as a sort of machine. Numbers go in, a decision comes out. Make borrowing cheaper and the machine, working properly, produces more building. There is nothing dishonest in that. It is a simplification, and simplifications are how anyone thinks about anything complicated. But it does mean that the last step in the chain — the step where somebody actually has to decide to build something — is the only step in the whole article that is not examined.

Then he found the sentence that turned an itch into an argument. On page 613, the authors ask what the government will do when its own borrowing costs fall. The government is the safest borrower in the country, and government debt is exactly what the central bank had been buying. So if anyone got the cheaper price, the government did. And the answer they give is that the government will not respond. Its spending plans, they write, should be unaffected. The reason has nothing to do with whether the money arrives. It arrives. The reason is that governments plan a long way ahead, so a short-term wobble in borrowing costs does not enter their thinking.

He remembers the feeling of reading that clearly. It was a small jolt. Here, in an article that had explained every failure to respond by money failing to arrive, was a case where the money definitely arrived and the response still did not come. And the explanation was about the way the decision gets made, rather than about the price. It was granted to exactly one actor, and then dropped.

He has since had to be honest with himself about how much that sentence really gives him, and it cost him the best version of this section. Read again, it is probably not saying anything unusual. Economists have a standard idea that a long-term planner responds to lasting changes and ignores passing ones. The word the authors use is 'cyclical', which means short-term and temporary. So they are very likely making an ordinary technical point, not a point about how organisations behave.

What survives is thinner than he first thought. It is this. A feature of the *decision itself* — how far ahead it looks — is allowed, once, to break the link between a cheaper price and more spending. Nobody then asks whether anything else about how a decision gets made could do the same. That is a reason to go and look. It is not evidence about what you find.

So he went and looked, and that is where the second reading comes in.

The article he turned to was 'Investment and Hysteresis', written in 1992 by Avinash Dixit, an economist at Princeton. Its subject is not central banks at all. It is about how businesses actually decide whether to build things.

Before that argument can start, one more idea needs unpacking, because every number in this section rests on it: what a company's own money costs it.

A company that builds a factory pays for it in one of two ways, and usually both. It borrows, and then it owes interest. Or it uses money its owners have put in, and those owners expect to be paid for it, because they could have put that money somewhere else instead. Either way the money has a price. Blend the two together and you get a single figure — what economists call the cost of capital, and what this account will call the cost of the company's money. It is the yearly percentage the project has to earn just to be worth doing at all. Earn less than that and the company would have done better leaving the money where it was.

Dixit opens with a fact about that figure which had been embarrassing his profession for years. If you ask companies what a project must promise before they will fund it, the answer is usually three or four times what their money costs them. On paper they are turning down profitable work. On paper they are behaving stupidly.

His argument is that they are not being stupid. They are doing something the textbook forgets to allow for.

The textbook offers a company two choices. Build now, or never build. Work out whether the project is worth more than it costs; if it is, go ahead. But in real life there is a third choice, and it is the one companies actually take most often. Wait.

Three things have to be true for waiting to be worth something, and the author found the list clarifying because he could test it against anything.

The first is that some of the money is gone the moment you spend it. Fit out a shop, drill a well, build a factory: you cannot change your mind next year and get that money back.

The second is that the world keeps surprising you, and you keep learning. You do not know what next year's trade will look like, but every month that passes tells you a little more.

The third is that the chance usually keeps. If you do not build this year, the opportunity is generally still there next year.

Put those three together and something follows that the author had never seen stated plainly. Waiting is not laziness. Waiting is worth money. Every month you hold off, you learn something, and you can still act on what you learn. And because the spending cannot be undone, acting early on poor information is a real gamble rather than a cheap experiment. So the bar a project must clear is higher than the textbook says. It has to cover its costs, and it has to pay for the chance you throw away by committing now.

Dixit then does something the author found more persuasive than any argument. He puts numbers on it. He asks how large this extra bar is for a business facing an ordinary amount of ups and downs. The answer is that the takings from a project must reach nearly double the level at which it would break even before building is the right move. Put the same thing another way. A company whose money genuinely costs it five per cent a year is behaving correctly if it refuses anything paying less than about nine. In a rougher trade — oil, metals, anything where prices swing hard — the figure rises to about sixteen per cent.

So the thing that had looked like corporate stupidity turns out to be arithmetic.

And here is the part that mattered most for the author's own argument. The size of that extra bar is not fixed. It grows as the world becomes more uncertain. In calm times it almost disappears, and the textbook is roughly right. In turbulent times it becomes enormous, and the textbook, in Dixit's own words, becomes totally misleading.

The author sat with that for a while, because of where it points. Quantitative easing is not used in calm times. It is used when interest rates have already been cut to nothing, and rates only get cut to nothing in a crisis. So the policy is used in exactly the conditions that make the extra bar largest. It is not a weaker version of an ordinary rate cut. It is a rate cut delivered in the one situation that blunts rate cuts.

Then he noticed something odder. Cheaper money is supposed to encourage building. But cheaper money also makes *waiting* cheaper. Think about what it costs a company to hold off for a year. It is the return the money would have earned in the meantime. If money is cheap, that forgone return is small, so holding off hurts less. Dixit says this directly: when the future matters more to you, waiting to learn about it is worth more. His own figures show the effect. At a cost of money of five per cent the extra bar is about double. At two per cent it is over two and a half times.

So a cut in borrowing costs pushes in two directions at once. It lowers the bar. It also fattens the extra bar sitting on top of it. The first effect wins, so the bar does come down — but part of the policy is being spent on making patience more attractive.

The author originally wanted to say something sharper here: that the bar comes down by less than you would expect. He has since dropped that, and the reason is worth recording, because it is a small lesson in how easily a person can flatter his own case. Whether the fall is large or small depends entirely on how you choose to measure it. Compare the two figures as percentage points and the bar falls by more than the cost of money does. Compare them as proportions and it falls by less. Both sums are correct. Neither is the true one. So the only claim he is entitled to is the plain one: in Dixit's model, the bar moves a great deal when the cost of money moves — and part of the movement runs backwards.

The second half of Dixit's article gave the author something he had not gone looking for, and it is the half that gives the article its odd title.

Suppose a business is already running and starts losing money. When should it close? The textbook says: close when you stop covering your day-to-day costs. Dixit says no. Staying open keeps alive the chance that trade recovers, and that chance is worth something too. So a company should absorb real losses before it shuts.

Which means there are now two levels rather than one. A high level at which it starts building, and a low level at which it gives up. Between them lies a wide stretch where the right thing to do is nothing at all. On Dixit's worked example that stretch is nine times wider than the textbook's. The comparison is the author's own, though. Dixit prints the four levels and calls the difference dramatic. It was the author who divided one by the other.

Now follow what a stretch that wide does. Push a business's takings up until it crosses the high level, so it builds. Then let takings fall all the way back to where they started. It does not close, because it is nowhere near the low level. The cause has completely reversed. The effect has not.

Dixit borrows a word from physics for this. Hysteresis describes an iron bar that stays magnetic after you switch off the current that magnetised it. The cause was temporary; the effect stayed.

And it works in reverse too, which is the part that bears on a policy trying to restart an economy. Push a business down through the low level so that it closes, and a recovery to normal does not bring it back — because coming back means paying the whole set-up cost again.

The author is careful about this one, because it is his strongest point and it rests on an assumption Dixit himself flags. To keep the argument clean, Dixit supposes that a stopped business is ruined instantly, so that pausing and quitting are the same thing. Where a firm can mothball a factory — shut it down but keep it in a state where it can be restarted cheaply — the point weakens. The author would rather say that than let his best argument stand on something his own source marks as a simplification.

There was one more idea in Dixit that changed how the author thought about the policy itself. It is not Dixit's own; he takes it from Ben Bernanke, who later ran the American central bank. It is called the bad news principle, and it says something surprising. When a business can wait, it is mainly the *bad* possible outcomes that affect whether it builds now. The good ones matter much less, because if things go well they will still be going well next year, and you will not have missed them. The only thing waiting protects you from is the bad news. So the bad news is most of what waiting is worth.

The author noticed that Dixit adds a qualification in the very next sentence, and since the qualification cuts against him he decided to carry it. The overall *chance* of things turning out well still counts, even if the size of the good outcome does not. And that overall chance is something a policy which supports spending can move. So this is not quite the clean split he first read it as.

Even so, it sent him back to what quantitative easing actually does. It works on the price of risk in financial markets. That phrase needs unpacking. Suppose you lend money for a long time, or lend it to someone who might not pay you back. You will want extra return for the worry. That extra is a price like any other, and quantitative easing pushes it down.

What it does not obviously work on is the particular fear that stops a company from committing. Will there still be customers in three years? Will the euro survive, or will countries start leaving it? Will this whole industry still exist? Those are different worries, and a central bank buying government debt reaches them only indirectly, if at all.

He wants to be careful here too, and being careful cost him ground again. He cannot claim the policy fails to touch that fear. Higher prices for shares and property do make some owners spend more, and one company's customers are other people's spending, so the fear is thinned a little. And buying government debt is obviously aimed at doubts about governments. So the honest version is about *how directly* rather than about *whether*. What a central bank reaches directly is the price of risk. What it reaches at one or two removes is the fear itself. That is weaker than what he wanted to say, and he has not measured it.

Dixit also offers a lesson that cuts the other way, and the author decided he had to include it because it damages him. Looking at Japanese companies of that period, Dixit notes that they invested boldly and hung on through losses, because the government cushioned their bad outcomes. Flip the bad news principle over and you get what Dixit calls the good news principle: the decision to *stay* is governed by the good possible outcomes, because that is what staying preserves. Which means a policy that props up prices and signals that the authorities will act is pointed at the right half for keeping firms alive. That hands quantitative easing a mechanism for the very thing the author was going to explain another way. He decided the discomfort was the point. It is what his own source says.

One more thing about Dixit had to be faced before any of this could be applied to a national policy. Everything so far is about a single business. Quantitative easing is aimed at a whole economy. Dixit does have a section showing that when many such businesses are put together in one market, the same two levels turn out to govern the market as a whole. But the author cannot lean on that as hard as he would like, because the businesses in that section are small and numerous and interchangeable — and the argument he is building applies to large companies. The bridge from one firm to the whole economy is narrower than he first assumed.

He does think the picture on the far side of that bridge is worth having, though, because it is what the whole argument amounts to. Dixit says that in a market of firms behaving this way you should expect long spells in which businesses make comfortable profits and nobody new arrives, and long spells in which businesses lose money and nobody closes. Look at such an economy on any given afternoon and it appears frozen. It is not. Nothing is broken, no policy has failed to arrive, and no rule is being flouted. That is simply what a market full of people waiting looks like from outside.

The third reading was quite different in character, and the author's reaction to it surprised him.

It was a study by two economists at the American central bank, Steven Sharpe and Gustavo Suarez, and it consisted of asking finance directors of large companies a direct question. If borrowing became cheaper, would you invest more? Most of them said no. Their plans would not change.

He had expected something dramatic. What he got was mundane, and the mundanity is what convinced him. Behind that answer sits an ordinary piece of office furniture. Most large companies write down a minimum return that any project must promise before it will be funded. It is a number in a written policy, applied by a board to proposals it cannot check for itself. It sits far above what the company's money actually costs. And surveys taken decades apart, in different countries, report it at similar heights, across a period when the cost of borrowing collapsed.

The author has to be honest about two things here, and both weaken him.

The first is that those surveys are not one continuous record. They are separate studies, in different places, done at different times by different people. He would be treating them as a series if he lined them up as a trend, and he will not.

The second is worse for him. He originally took the finance directors' answer as evidence for his case, and it is not. They were asked whether their *investment plans* would change. That is not the same question as whether the *number in the policy* would change. A company sitting well below its bar can honestly say its plans would not change while its written bar is quietly falling. So the study that sent him looking cannot be used to support what he ended up arguing. He kept it in the account because it is where the thought started, not because it proves anything.

There is a third thing, and it is the least comfortable. Sharpe and Suarez have a favourite explanation of their own finding, and it is neither Dixit's nor the author's. They lean towards companies having plenty of spare cash already, so that the price of borrowing simply does not come into it. That is a third possible answer, the author has not tackled it anywhere, and it comes from the very people whose study set him off.

Still, something is holding that number up. This is where the author's own contribution comes in — and it is worth saying straight away that the contribution is smaller than the word suggests. The idea he is about to set out is not one he invented. What he has done is put it to a use he has not seen it put to.

Dixit already explains why the number is high. He does it well, and the author concedes it entirely. What the author disputes is what *kind* of number it is.

In Dixit's account it is worked out from things outside the company: the cost of the company's money, how choppy its market is, how fast that market is growing. It is an outside price wearing a disguise. If the outside price moves, this number moves.

The author's suggestion is that in a real business it is anchored to something inside instead.

The reasoning starts from an assumption he states openly, because it is doing a lot of work and it might be wrong. In a large company, the thing that runs out first is not money. It is management. There are always more ideas than there are people capable of running them properly. Large companies are split into separately run parts — divisions — each with someone in charge of arguing for it. Those managers push their own projects and present them optimistically, because that is what advocating for your division looks like, and the board cannot check what it is told. So the board's real problem is not working out what each project is worth. It is choosing between them. Which of these do we actually have the people to do properly?

A minimum return set to solve *that* problem is tied to the next project in the queue — the best thing the company would have to drop in order to take this one on. That is an internal quantity. It depends on how many good ideas the business is generating and how many capable people it has spare. A fall in the return on government debt does not lengthen anybody's working week.

There is a hole in that reasoning and the author would rather point at it himself. What is actually scarce, on his own account, is his managers' time. But the tool the company uses to ration it is a percentage return on money. Those are not the same thing at all. A large, simple purchase can swallow a lot of money and very little management. A small, fiddly reorganisation can swallow the reverse. So a single percentage applied to every project is a crude way to share out people's attention.

He thinks the crudeness is telling rather than damning. A rule that a board can apply to proposals it cannot verify has to be simple, uniform and hard to argue with, and being crude is part of how it does that job. But he accepts that a critic could read exactly the same fact the other way, and conclude that he has named the wrong scarce thing.

The next question, and the first one any reader will ask, is why the company does not simply hire more managers — especially now that money is cheap.

The answer is not the author's. It comes from Edith Penrose, who wrote about this in 1959, and he should say that he knows her argument at second hand rather than from her book. New managers cannot just be bought and switched on. They have to be trained and absorbed by the managers already there, and that takes the time of exactly the people who are already stretched. So the constraint loosens over years, not over the months on which a rate cut is meant to work.

The author accepts that answer, and then had to accept what it costs him. If the constraint loosens over years, then the minimum return it sets should also drift down over years. That is not *no* movement. It is slow, partial movement — which is a much smaller claim than the one he started with, and uncomfortably close to a simpler explanation he cannot rule out.

That simpler explanation is this. Any published rule is slow to change, whatever it was based on, because a number that moves every month stops being a rule and becomes something to argue about. On that story the minimum return could be exactly what Dixit says it is — an outside price marked up — and still sit still for years, because nobody has got round to recalculating it.

The author raises that rival himself. He also has to report the best evidence available on which of them is right, and it does not settle in his favour. A recent study finds that these minimum returns do follow the cost of money, but weakly, and over years. He has not read that study; he knows of it through a summary of the field, and he marks it that way rather than dressing it up. What he can say is that slow partial movement fits the simpler rival at least as well as it fits him.

There is one more weakness in his own reasoning that he could not close. Suppose the queue of projects is ranked by how valuable each one is. A fall in the cost of money makes every project look a bit better, and it does not lift them all equally, so the order shuffles. Which means the next project in the queue is not quite independent of interest rates after all. His answer is that in practice the supply of ideas is set by the organisation rather than by the rate. He believes that. He cannot show it.

So the version of his idea he can defend is smaller than the version he wanted. Not that the number never moves. Rather, that it moves on the company's clock rather than the central bank's — changing as the pipeline of ideas and the stock of managers change, over years. For a policy meant to work inside a downturn, that is close enough to failure to matter. It is not the same as never.

Before turning to the last of his four readings, one more episode belongs in this account. Of everything he did, it is the thing that taught him most about his own thinking.

An argument like this is worth little unless there is some way of finding out that it is wrong. So the author asked himself what he would go and measure. His first instinct was obvious to him. Sort companies by how much of their spending cannot be undone. A steelworks sits at one end, a firm that mostly buys resaleable vans at the other. Then see which sort responds less when borrowing gets cheaper.

Then he worked through what Dixit's argument actually says, and found that his instinct was pointed at the wrong decision. The size of the unrecoverable spending raises the bar for building, but it does not change how much that bar moves when money gets cheaper. What it *does* change is the other decision. A company with more sunk in a project will endure worse losses before closing it, because it has more to protect. So the amount you cannot get back matters enormously for when a firm gives up, and rather little for when it starts.

He then had to correct his correction, which is the part he found most instructive. He had first concluded that Dixit's argument said his test would not work. It does not say that. Dixit's model treats spending as either recoverable or not, with nothing in between, so it contains no measure of how *much* you get back on resale — which is what the test was actually sorting on. The model is silent on the question rather than hostile to it. There is a difference, and he had blurred it in his own favour.

He found the whole episode genuinely useful, and not in the way he expected. The source did not tell him his instinct was wrong. It told him what his instinct had actually been an instinct about.

There is one more question about his own two ideas that he could not settle, and he would rather leave it open than answer it in his own favour. If the queue inside a company sets a bar, and waiting sets another bar, which one binds? Two bars on the same decision cannot both matter; only the higher one does. His suggestion is that they work at different stages. The queue decides which proposals get into the budget at all. Waiting decides when an approved project is actually begun, and when a running one is stopped. But he notes that this arrangement cuts against his own conclusion. If waiting has the last word on when a project starts, then cheaper money can still set off projects already sitting in the budget. In which case the policy reaches the decision after all.

The last reading brought him back to where he started, and changed the meaning of something he had already read once.

The article was 'Quantitative Easing: A Sceptical Survey', by Christopher Martin and Costas Milas, published in the same issue as the one he began with. It gathers up the studies of what the policy actually achieved and reaches a two-part conclusion. The studies say quantitative easing worked: they put it at raising the country's rate of growth by around one to three per cent, with a similar effect on prices. It kept the downturn from being worse than it was. But by itself, the authors write, it was not strong enough to start a recovery. They add that this conclusion is tentative, and the author should have carried that word with him from the first reading rather than picking it up later.

The first time he read that, he treated it as a result. Here is what the numbers say; write it down and move on.

Reading it again after Dixit, he could not let it alone. Why would one policy be good at stopping a fall and bad at starting a rise? Those are not obviously different jobs. And he felt genuinely uncomfortable at that moment — not with the article, but with himself, for having read the sentence once already and never asked whether there was a reason behind it.

He can now offer a reason. The two jobs pull on the same thing in opposite directions. Stopping a collapse means keeping markets working and holding up the good possible outcomes, and by Dixit's good news principle that is exactly what keeps a struggling firm from closing. Starting a recovery means getting companies to commit money they cannot get back, which is exactly what fear makes them least willing to do. So the policy is used in the situation where the first job is easiest and the second is hardest. It steadies without starting. And for firms that shut down on the way through, restoring conditions to normal does not bring them back at all.

Two admissions belong with that, and the author would rather make them than have them made for him.

The first is that part of the explanation just given is his and not Dixit's. That propping up the good outcomes works *best* when things are frightening, and that the two jobs therefore pull apart, are steps he has added. Dixit's own text does not contain them.

The second is that Martin and Milas offer their own explanation, and it may simply be the right one. They say quantitative easing is a weak tool: the largest programmes did roughly what cutting interest rates by two or three percentage points would have done. A dose that size might well be enough to cushion a fall and not enough to reverse one. That is a complete answer and it needs nothing from the author. His account differs in principle — it says the same dose would work better in calm conditions — but he cannot test that difference, because the policy is only ever used in a crisis. So the two readings are rivals in principle and, on the evidence available, cannot be told apart in practice.

Martin and Milas also gave him a boundary he had to accept. They report that quantitative easing does not appear to have affected the interest rates facing small and medium-sized companies and households. For one kind of borrowing, things got worse anyway — not because of the policy, but in spite of it. That is borrowing with nothing pledged against it — no property, no equipment the lender can take if you fail to repay. Rates on that kind rose in 2008 and 2009. The reason is that lenders raised their extra charge for risky borrowers faster than the central bank's rate came down. So for those borrowers there is nothing left for the author to explain. The money never reached them, and the original article's own caveat covers them completely. Whatever he has to say applies only to large companies that did see the cheaper price — which is, as it happens, where his argument about internal queues is strongest anyway.

And they gave him a warning he has tried to take seriously. Their own article says the research on quantitative easing is thin, uses similar methods, and mostly comes from central banks — which are not neutral parties. The finding he treats as a fact to be explained comes largely from that research. And they themselves call their conclusion tentative.

There is one further objection the author has to record, because it is the strongest one against him and he cannot answer it. He did not see it while he was reading. It surfaced afterwards, when he went back to check the sentence that had started the whole thing off, and read it more carefully than he had the first time.

Go back to that sentence about the government on page 613. If the reason a long-term planner ignores a change is that the change is *temporary*, then the simplest explanation of why companies did not respond to quantitative easing is that they thought it was temporary too. Rates were pushed down, everyone expected them to be pushed back up, and nobody rebuilt a factory around a rate they did not expect to last. That explanation needs nothing from Dixit and nothing from management. It sits entirely inside the original article's own framework.

The author has three things to say to it, and none of them is a refutation. The two stories are not exclusive; a change that looked permanent would still meet a gate inside the firm. The two also make different predictions, because the temporary story says companies would respond fully to a change they believed would last. And in fact Dixit's own reasoning strengthens the temporary story rather than competing with it — a change you think might not last is precisely the sort of thing that makes waiting attractive. Which of the two explanations does more of the work is a question this argument does not settle.

There is also a limit that Dixit himself sets, and it is a real one. If several firms are racing for the same opportunity — one site, one licence — then waiting is not possible, because a rival will take it. In that case the textbook is right after all. The argument applies to investment a company can take its time over, not to a race.

So where the author ended up is narrower than where he set out, and he thinks the narrowing is the honest part.

He is not saying quantitative easing does not work. He has not disputed that it moved prices in financial markets, which is the best-established finding in the whole body of research. He is not saying the theory in Bowdler and Radia is false. He is saying it is incomplete, in one specific place. Their chain contains careful reasoning about what can go wrong at every stage except the last one — the stage where a quantity finally has to change, because somebody in a room has to say yes.

Two things belong there.

The first is Dixit's. When spending cannot be undone and the future is unknown, the bar a project must clear is higher than the textbook says. And it rises as the world becomes more frightening. So a policy used in frightening times is working against the largest version of that bar. That is not the author's idea. It is well-established economics, published in a general economics journal, and simply absent from the article he began with. He is mostly reporting it.

The second is the one he has been arguing for, and here he has to be precise about what is his. The idea that a company's minimum return is a rationing tool rather than a calculation is not his either; there is a body of published work on it that he knows of and has not read. What he would claim is the use he has put it to: applying it to the question of why a central bank's money does not turn into buildings. And even that he holds loosely, because he cannot at present name a single observation that would tell his version apart from the simpler story that any written rule is slow to be rewritten. He believes his version is right. He is aware that believing something is not the same as showing it.

What he finds most interesting is not the conclusion but where the gap came from. It is not a mistake in anybody's reasoning. It is a boundary. A model of the whole economy hands off to a decision it does not model. And the study of how that decision actually gets made sits in a different journal, on a different reading list, and — as it happens — in the other half of the same degree.
