PPrimer: what the paper assumes you already know

This paper asks one question: when should a business take a big, hard-to-undo step, such as building a factory, and when should it wait? The paper's answer overturned the textbooks, and it needs three kinds of background: a little money arithmetic, a little chance, and a little about how economists draw markets. This primer builds all of it from nothing, in order. The sections on sunk costs (P2), Marshall's rules (P4) and options (P7) matter most; the maths toolkit (P8) will make the equations feel much smaller than they look.

P1. Money, interest, and why £100 later is worth less than £100 now

Start with money (anything people accept as payment: notes, coins, and the numbers in bank accounts). If you lend money, you expect a little extra back. That extra is interest (the extra money a borrower pays a lender on top of what was borrowed), and the interest rate (interest for one year, written as a percentage of the amount lent: lend £100 at 5 per cent and you get £105 back a year later) says how much extra per year. 'Per cent (out of every hundred: 5 per cent of £200 is £10)' just means 'out of every hundred', and the gap between two rates is measured in percentage points (the gap between two percentages: 9 per cent is 4 percentage points above 5 per cent).

Interest creates a rule this whole paper leans on: money later is worth less than money now, because money now could be earning interest in the meantime. Working out what a future sum is worth today is called discounting (shrinking future money into today's terms, using an interest rate, because money now could be earning interest in the meantime), and the rate used for the shrinking is the discount rate (the interest rate used to shrink future money into today's terms; in this paper it is the Greek letter ρ, said 'rho'). What the future money is worth today is its present value (what a future sum or stream of payments is worth today, after discounting).

Worked example: the one formula to keep
At an interest rate of 5 per cent a year, £105 next year is worth £100 today, because £100 today would grow into £105. Now the version the paper uses constantly. Suppose a project pays £5 a year, every year, for ever. How much is that stream worth today, at 5 per cent? Answer: £100, because £100 in the bank at 5 per cent would pay you the same £5 a year for ever. The rule: a payment of R every year for ever, at discount rate ρ, has a present value of R divided by ρ. Check it: 5 ÷ 0.05 = 100. When you meet 'R/ρ' in the paper, it is this: the whole infinite stream, priced today.

P2. Firms, projects, and the three kinds of cost

A firm (any business: a corner shop, a farm, an oil company) is any business. Investment (spending money now to build or buy something that will earn money later: a factory, a machine, an oil well) is spending now to earn later, and a single investment decision, one factory, one well, one shop, is a project (one particular investment: this factory, this oil well, this shop). The money a project brings in is revenue (the money coming in from selling; in this paper 'net revenue' means revenue after paying the running costs); the money it uses up is its cost (the money a project uses up; economists split costs into kinds, and the kinds are the whole point here); what is left is profit (revenue minus cost; an 'operating profit' counts only the running costs, and an 'operating loss' is a negative one).

Now the split that drives the paper. A variable cost (a running cost that stops when you stop producing: ingredients, electricity, hourly wages) stops when you stop. A fixed cost (a cost that does not change with how much you produce, such as rent or the interest on set-up money; some fixed costs can be escaped by shutting down for good (a lease you can end), others are sunk (money already spent on the plant), and the paper turns on which) does not vary with output. And a sunk cost (money that is gone for good once spent, whatever you do next: you cannot get it back by stopping, selling up or changing your mind) can never be recovered at all. Fitting out a shop, drilling a well, building a factory: once spent, that money is sunk. The paper's first big claim is that almost every real investment has a sunk part, and that this changes everything.

Money tied up in a business is capital (money tied up in a business, or the machines and buildings that money bought). The return that money could have earned elsewhere is the cost of capital (the return the money tied up in a project could have earned in its next best use; a project must beat this to be worth doing). Firms often demand more than that before acting: the return a firm insists a project must promise before going ahead is its hurdle rate (the minimum rate of return a firm demands from a project before it will invest; observed hurdle rates are far above the cost of capital, and explaining that gap is one of this paper's aims). A project's net worth (in this paper, a project's net present value: the present value of what it will earn minus what it costs to set up; in everyday use, a person's or firm's assets minus debts) is its discounted future earnings minus the set-up cost. Undoing an investment, closing and pulling out, is disinvestment (pulling out of an investment: closing the plant, leaving the market).

P3. Markets: price, demand and supply

A market (wherever buyers and sellers meet and a price forms: the market for fish, for oil, for flats) is wherever buyers and sellers trade, and the price (what one unit sells for in the market; the paper's symbol for it is P) is what a unit sells for. Demand (how much buyers want to buy at each possible price; drawn as a downward-sloping line called the demand curve, because at higher prices people buy less) is the buyers' side; supply (how much sellers offer at each possible price; drawn as an upward-sloping line called the supply curve, because higher prices tempt more production) is the sellers' side. Economists draw both as lines on a chart of price against quantity (the amount bought and sold; the paper's symbol for it is Q), and where the lines cross is where the market settles.

A market with many small firms, none big enough to move the price alone, is called competition (a market with many small firms, none big enough to move the price on its own; each firm must take the market price as given), and such firms are 'price-takers'. New firms joining a market is entry (new firms joining a market and starting to produce); firms leaving is exit (firms leaving a market and stopping production for good). One seller alone is a monopoly (a market with only one seller); a handful of big sellers is an oligopoly (a market with only a few big sellers, each watching the others' moves). One more drawing tool for later: a firm's marginal cost (what one extra unit costs to produce; it usually rises as a firm pushes its capacity, and the rising part acts as the firm's supply curve) is the cost of one more unit.

P4. Marshall's textbook rules: the world this paper attacks

Alfred Marshall (the Cambridge economist (1842–1924) whose Principles of Economics set the frame still used in textbooks; 'Marshallian' means 'following Marshall's textbook analysis') built the frame the textbooks still use. He split time in two. In the short run (Marshall's near term: the period in which a firm cannot change its fixed commitments, only its output) a firm can only vary output. In the long run (Marshall's far term: the period long enough for firms to build, enter or leave, so every commitment can be changed) firms can build, enter and leave.

Two averages then set the rules. Long run average cost (total cost per unit once every cost is counted, including the interest on the money sunk in setting up; Marshall's entry line) counts everything, including a fair return on the set-up money. Average variable cost (the running cost per unit only, ignoring the set-up money altogether; Marshall's exit line) counts only the running costs. The Marshallian (following Marshall's textbook analysis: enter when price covers long run average cost, exit when price fails to cover average variable cost) rules say: if the price covers long run average cost, invest, expand, enter; if the price falls below average variable cost, stop, suspend, exit; in between, sit still. The paper opens with these rules, then spends twenty-five pages showing that a world with chance in it obeys neither.

One more word from this world: equilibrium (a state the market has settled into, where no one who could change their behaviour wants to; prices and plans fit together) is where things settle when everyone has adjusted, and the status quo (the way things currently are; 'maintaining the status quo' means changing nothing) is simply the way things now stand.

P5. Chance: probability, expected value, and two kinds of risk

A probability (how likely something is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain): a fair coin lands heads with probability 0.5) measures how likely something is. When an outcome is unknown, economists average over the possibilities: the expected value (the probability-weighted average of the possible outcomes: multiply each outcome by its probability and add up; also called the mean, or just 'the expected' amount) multiplies each outcome by its probability and adds up. The spread of possible outcomes around that average has three names you will meet: the variance (the average of the squared distances of the possible outcomes from their mean, so a measure of spread in squared units; the paper writes it σ², said 'sigma squared') (the statistician's spread measure), its square root the standard deviation (the square root of the variance: a spread measure in the same units as the thing itself; the paper's symbol is σ, said 'sigma'), and the everyday word volatility (how much and how fast something bounces around; measured by the standard deviation of its movements). A coefficient of variation (the standard deviation written as a percentage of the average: a spread of 10 on an average of 100 is a coefficient of variation of 10 per cent) rescales the spread as a percentage of the average, so different-sized things can be compared.

Worked example
A ticket pays £10 with probability 0.5, and £0 otherwise. Its expected value is 0.5 × £10 + 0.5 × £0 = £5. Someone who would pay exactly £5 for that ticket, caring only about the average, is risk-neutral (caring only about the expected value of an outcome, not about its spread: paying exactly £5 for a half-chance of £10). Someone who would pay less, because the spread itself hurts, is risk-averse (disliking spread itself: preferring a sure £4 to a half-chance of £10, even though the average of the gamble is higher). This paper assumes risk-neutral investors on purpose, to prove its effect has nothing to do with disliking risk.

The whole menu of possibilities and their probabilities is a distribution (the full menu of possible outcomes with a probability attached to each; pictures of it show which outcomes are likely and which are rare). A distribution is symmetric (balanced around the middle: outcomes the same distance above and below the average are equally likely) if up and down are equally likely, and lopsided if not. Two directions get names: downside risk (the chance of bad outcomes: the lower part of the distribution) and upside potential (the chance of good outcomes: the upper part of the distribution). Keep those two apart; one of the paper's neatest results is that only one of them matters at a time.

Last word here: economists use uncertainty (not knowing how things will turn out; in this paper it simply means the future is random, with ongoing surprises arriving over time) loosely for 'the future is random'. A process that unfolds randomly through time is called stochastic (unfolding randomly through time; a 'stochastic environment' is one where new surprises keep arriving).

P6. Random walks: how the paper makes revenue move

The paper needs a rule for how a project's revenue wanders through time. It uses a random walk (a path that moves by a fresh random step each period, up or down, with no memory: where it goes next depends only on where it is now): each period, revenue takes a fresh random step. If the steps are equal percentages rather than equal amounts, up 10 per cent or down 10 per cent rather than up £10 or down £10, the walk is called geometric (moving in equal percentage steps rather than equal amounts; a 'geometric series' is a sequence where each term is the previous one times a fixed ratio). A tilt in the steps, up slightly likelier than down, gives the walk a trend (a steady underlying tilt in a wandering path: the direction it drifts on average; the paper's symbol for the trend growth rate is μ, said 'mu').

Make the steps tiny and rapid and the walk smooths into the mathematician's idealised version, Brownian motion (the mathematician's idealised random walk with infinitely small, infinitely rapid steps; named after the botanist Robert Brown, who watched tiny particles from pollen grains jiggling in water). The percentage-step version is geometric Brownian motion (Brownian motion in percentage terms: the thing wanders by proportional steps, so it can never go below zero; the standard model for prices); because the steps are proportional, such a path can fall towards zero but never below it, which suits prices and revenues. A sequence of values through time, revenue by week, a price by day, is a time series (a sequence of values through time: a price recorded day by day, revenue week by week).

Two helpers from the maths cupboard. The logarithm (the exponent a fixed base must be raised to in order to give your number; taking logs turns multiplying into adding, so equal percentage steps become equal-sized steps) of a number is the power ten (or another base) must be raised to in order to give it; its party trick is turning multiplication into addition, so a path moving in equal percentage steps has a logarithm moving in equal absolute steps. And the normal distribution (the bell-shaped distribution that piles up around the average; sums of many small independent surprises tend towards it) is the famous bell curve, which shows up whenever many small independent surprises add up. The paper needs one sentence's worth of each, no more.

P7. Financial options: the analogy the whole field is built on

A share (a small slice of ownership of a company, tradeable on a stock market; also called a stock; it may pay its owner a regular slice of profit) (or stock) is a slice of a company; the regular payment it makes to its owner is a dividend (a regular payment a company makes to its shareholders out of profits). An option (a contract giving the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell something at a preset price; you can use it or let it sit) is a contract giving a right without an obligation. A call option (an option to BUY: the right, but not the obligation, to buy a share at a preset price called the strike price or exercise price) is the right to buy a share at a preset price, the strike price (the preset price written into an option; also called the exercise price; using the option is 'exercising' it). Using the right is called exercising (using an option: actually buying (or selling) at the preset price written into the contract) the option. An American call option (a call option that can be exercised at any time of the owner's choosing, not just on one final date; the kind real investment resembles) can be exercised whenever its owner likes; the date an option dies is its expiry date (the date an option contract ends; after it, the right is gone; an option with no such date is 'perpetual').

Worked example
You hold a call option on a share, strike price £50. The share trades at £60 today. Exercising now nets you £10, and that £10 is the option's intrinsic value (what exercising an option right now would net you: the current price minus the strike price, when that gap is positive). But should you exercise now? Perhaps not. The share might rise further, and if it falls you can simply not exercise; holding on keeps that choice alive. The extra worth of keeping the choice alive, over and above the £10, is the option's time value (the extra worth of an option above its intrinsic value, from keeping the choice alive while the future unfolds; the paper also calls it the 'holding premium'). The heart of this paper is one idea: a firm able to wait before investing holds exactly this kind of option, and the option's time value is the value of waiting.

  • variance the average of the squared distances of the possible outcomes from their mean, so a measure of spread in squared units; the paper writes it σ², said 'sigma squared'
  • standard deviation the square root of the variance: a spread measure in the same units as the thing itself; the paper's symbol is σ, said 'sigma'
  • volatility how much and how fast something bounces around; measured by the standard deviation of its movements
  • coefficient of variation the standard deviation written as a percentage of the average: a spread of 10 on an average of 100 is a coefficient of variation of 10 per cent
  • risk-neutral caring only about the expected value of an outcome, not about its spread: paying exactly £5 for a half-chance of £10
  • risk-averse disliking spread itself: preferring a sure £4 to a half-chance of £10, even though the average of the gamble is higher
  • distribution the full menu of possible outcomes with a probability attached to each; pictures of it show which outcomes are likely and which are rare
  • symmetric balanced around the middle: outcomes the same distance above and below the average are equally likely
  • downside risk the chance of bad outcomes: the lower part of the distribution
  • upside potential the chance of good outcomes: the upper part of the distribution
  • uncertainty not knowing how things will turn out; in this paper it simply means the future is random, with ongoing surprises arriving over time
  • stochastic unfolding randomly through time; a 'stochastic environment' is one where new surprises keep arriving
  • random walk a path that moves by a fresh random step each period, up or down, with no memory: where it goes next depends only on where it is now
  • geometric moving in equal percentage steps rather than equal amounts; a 'geometric series' is a sequence where each term is the previous one times a fixed ratio
  • trend a steady underlying tilt in a wandering path: the direction it drifts on average; the paper's symbol for the trend growth rate is μ, said 'mu'
  • Brownian motion the mathematician's idealised random walk with infinitely small, infinitely rapid steps; named after the botanist Robert Brown, who watched tiny particles from pollen grains jiggling in water
  • geometric Brownian motion Brownian motion in percentage terms: the thing wanders by proportional steps, so it can never go below zero; the standard model for prices
  • time series a sequence of values through time: a price recorded day by day, revenue week by week
  • logarithm the exponent a fixed base must be raised to in order to give your number; taking logs turns multiplying into adding, so equal percentage steps become equal-sized steps
  • normal distribution the bell-shaped distribution that piles up around the average; sums of many small independent surprises tend towards it
  • share a small slice of ownership of a company, tradeable on a stock market; also called a stock; it may pay its owner a regular slice of profit
  • dividend a regular payment a company makes to its shareholders out of profits
  • option a contract giving the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell something at a preset price; you can use it or let it sit
  • call option an option to BUY: the right, but not the obligation, to buy a share at a preset price called the strike price or exercise price
  • strike price the preset price written into an option; also called the exercise price; using the option is 'exercising' it
  • exercising using an option: actually buying (or selling) at the preset price written into the contract
  • American call option a call option that can be exercised at any time of the owner's choosing, not just on one final date; the kind real investment resembles
  • expiry date the date an option contract ends; after it, the right is gone; an option with no such date is 'perpetual'
  • intrinsic value what exercising an option right now would net you: the current price minus the strike price, when that gap is positive
  • time value the extra worth of an option above its intrinsic value, from keeping the choice alive while the future unfolds; the paper also calls it the 'holding premium'
  • subscript a small label hanging below a symbol, as in R₁: it names WHICH R is meant (R at time 1), it is not arithmetic
  • function a rule turning one number into another: V(R) is the rule that says what the project opportunity is worth when revenue is R; said 'V of R'
  • power repeated multiplication: R³ is R × R × R; in R^β, said 'R to the beta', the raised letter is the exponent; powers grow curves that steepen as they rise
  • exponent the raised symbol in a power: the 2 in σ², the β in R^β; it says how many times to multiply
  • square root the number that gives your number when multiplied by itself: the square root of 9 is 3; written with the √ sign
  • quadratic equation an equation whose unknown appears squared and no higher, such as x² − x − 6 = 0; it has two solutions, called its roots
  • roots the solutions of an equation: the values of the unknown that make it true
  • graph a drawing of a function: one number along the bottom, the other up the side, and a line showing how they relate
  • horizontal axis the flat direction of a chart, along the bottom; in the paper's figures it carries the revenue R or the quantity Q
  • vertical axis the upright direction of a chart, up the side; in the paper's figures it carries value V or price P
  • slope how steeply a line climbs: the rise for each unit of run; a slope of 1/ρ means each £1 of extra revenue adds £1/ρ of value
  • convex curving upwards like a bowl: its slope keeps increasing as you move right
  • derivative the slope of a curve at a point: how fast the function is changing just there; written with a dash, so V′(R) is the slope of V at R, and V″(R) is the slope of the slope
  • tangential touching at a point with the same slope there: kissing, not crossing; 'tangentially' is the adverb
  • differential equation an equation that pins down a function by relating it to its own slopes; solving one means finding the function that obeys it
  • closed-form solution an answer you can write as an explicit formula; when no such formula exists, the answer must be hunted numerically by computer
  • limiting case what happens to a formula when one of its ingredients is pushed to an extreme, such as zero or infinity; a quick way to test whether a formula behaves sensibly
  • infinity larger than any number; 'goes to infinity' means grows without any limit
  • Journal of Economic Perspectives a journal of the American Economic Association, started in 1987, that asks leading economists to explain research fields in plain terms, for economists outside the speciality; papers in it carry no abstract
  • citation a pointer to another work, such as 'Bernanke (1983)'; the full details sit in the reference list at the end of the paper
  • et al. Latin for 'and others', used when a work has several authors
  • footnote a numbered note at the bottom of the page; this paper has thirteen, and several carry real content, including whole numerical results
  • working paper research circulated before a journal has accepted it; several of this paper's references were still working papers in 1992
  • appendix a section after the main text holding the heavier material; this paper's printed appendix derives its key formulas, and Dixit also kept an 'additional Appendix' he would post to anyone who wrote to him

SThe story we will use: Meera and the chip shop on Saltmarsh pier

One story runs through this guide. It is introduced here once, in full; a cream box at the head of every section picks it up and tells you where you are. The story is a tool for following the argument. The numbers and claims always come from the paper.

The story

Meera and the chip shop on Saltmarsh pier

Meera has £20,000 in the Saltmarsh Savings Bank, earning a steady 5 per cent, and an eye on the empty shop unit at the end of the pier. The pier company will let her have it whenever she likes. But fitting out a chip shop, fryers, counters, signage bolted to a salty pier, costs the whole £20,000, and not a penny comes back if she ever changes her mind. Spent is spent.

Her first sum is the one the textbook teaches. The £20,000 in the bank pays £1,000 a year. So the shop is worth opening the moment a pier chippy's takings, after fish, potatoes and wages, beat £1,000 a year. That break-even level is her back-of-envelope line: cross it, invest.

But trade on the pier is not steady. A hot August doubles takings; a washed-out one halves them; fashions for the seafront come and go. Takings drift by percentages, up a little likelier or down a little likelier, no one knows. And here is the thing Meera slowly realises: because the fee is sunk, and the future keeps arriving, and the unit is not going anywhere, there is real value in simply watching one more season. If trade turns bad, she has lost nothing but a summer; if it turns good, she can still open. Waiting lets her dodge the bad futures while keeping the good ones. So her rule is not the break-even line. She pins a card above the till she does not yet own: open only when takings reach £H a week, a level far above break-even.

Suppose she opens. The pier has one more cruelty. A chip shop that closes for a season is a chip shop the salt air ruins overnight: reopening means paying the whole £20,000 again. So closing is for keeps, and the same logic runs in reverse. When takings sag below the weekly bill for fish and wages, she does not close at once; the shop, kept alive, is her only claim on the good summers that may come back. She pins a second card: close only if takings sink to £L, a level far below the running bill. Between £L and £H lies a wide stretch of trade where the right move is to do nothing at all: not open if shut, not shut if open.

Now watch the pier as a whole, and a strange pattern appears. In the blazing summer when takings crossed £H, chip shops sprang up all along the boardwalk. Trade later fell all the way back to where it began, and the shops stayed. The cause came and went; the effect remained. Saltmarsh's physics teacher has a name for this from her own subject: run a current round an iron bar and it magnetises; switch the current off and some magnetism stays. Hysteresis.

Two rivalries finish the tale. Down the coast lies Greywick, whose council quietly tops up any shopkeeper's bad season. Greywick keepers, their downside covered, see little point in waiting and pile in early; and once in, they hang on through winters that would empty Saltmarsh pier. And on Saltmarsh pier itself, Meera is not alone: a whole queue of identical would-be keepers watches the same notice board, so the posted price of a fish supper can never rise far before new shops open, nor fall far before old ones quit. The band between those bounds is wide, and inside it the pier looks lazy, profitable shops with no newcomers, loss-making shops that refuse to die, while behaving exactly as it should.

The paper is this story with the numbers filled in: how far above break-even £H sits, how far below the running bill £L sits, and why the answers embarrass the textbook.

Testing the story

Every mapping was checked for direction. Waiting raises the bar for opening (H above break-even) and lowers the bar for closing (L below the running bill), exactly as the paper's triggers move. The bank's 5 per cent plays the discount rate, so break-even takings equal the interest on the fee, which is the paper's M = ρK. The ruined fryers give 'total and immediate rusting', the paper's own phrase for why suspension equals abandonment. Greywick's covered downside runs the paper's bad news principle in the right direction: less downside risk, less value in waiting, keener investment, and, on the way out, more upside hope, so later exit. One thing the story leaves alone on purpose: the sizes of the effects, which are the paper's numerical results, done in the open in the Check-the-numbers boxes.

The story so far
Where we are: Meera has not appeared yet. The paper opens by showing that real shopkeepers all along the coast ignore the textbook's break-even rules: they demand takings far above break-even before opening, and they hang on long after takings collapse. The puzzle comes first; the explanation follows.
1.1The economic theory of investment under competitive conditions rests on the foundations of Marshall's analysis of long and short run equilibria.

The paper opens with the received wisdom. The textbook theory of investment in competitive markets (primer P3 and P4) stands on Alfred Marshall (the Victorian economist whose framework the textbooks still use)'s split between the short run and the long run, and on where markets settle in each.

Note on the original

In print, the opening 'T' is a giant decorated capital spanning four lines: a drop cap. The word 'equilibria' is the plural of equilibrium.

1.2If price exceeds long run average cost, this induces existing firms to expand, and new ones to enter.

Marshall's first rule, from primer P4: when the market price covers the full cost per unit, including a fair return on the set-up money, firms already in the market grow and outsiders join.

1.3If price falls below average variable cost, then firms suspend operations or even exit from the market.

Marshall's second rule: when the price fails to cover even the day-to-day running cost per unit, firms pause production or leave for good. Between the two lines, do nothing. Hold these two rules in mind; the whole paper is an attack on them.

1.4Reality is very different.

Four words announcing the paper's method: check the textbook against the world.

1.5Firms invest in projects that they expect to yield a return in excess of a required or "hurdle" rate.

Real firms use a different rule. Before starting a project, they demand that its expected return clears a hurdle rate (the minimum return a firm insists on before investing) set well above anything the textbook would ask.

1.6Observers of business practice find that such hurdle rates are three or four times the cost of capital.1

And the hurdle is set startlingly high: three or four times the cost of capital (the return the money could earn elsewhere). The textbook says invest whenever the return beats the cost of capital, so on paper these firms are leaving profitable projects on the table. The raised 1 points to footnote 1, which gives the evidence.

Key point
This is the paper's founding fact. Firms behave as if the entry bar sits far above break-even. The paper's job is to show that this is not stupidity but arithmetic.

1.13The hurdle rate appropriate for investment with systematic risk will exceed the riskless rate, but it seems hard to justify the large discrepancies observed.

The obvious defence fails. Yes, a project whose fortunes swing with the whole economy carries systematic risk (risk that moves with the whole economy and so cannot be spread away by holding many different assets; investors demand extra return for bearing it), and deserves a hurdle above the riskless rate. But not this far above: the standard risk adjustments cannot stretch to three or four times.

1.14On the downside, firms stay in business for lengthy periods while absorbing operating losses, and price can fall substantially below average variable cost without inducing disinvestment or exit.

The exit rule is broken too, in the mirror-image way. Firms losing money month after month, with price below the running-cost line, stay open anyway, and do not disinvestment (pulling out: closing the plant, leaving the market) or leave.

2Timing of Investment and the Value of Waiting (pages 108–109)

The story so far
Where we are: Meera notices the three things that make her decision hard: the fitting-out fee is unrecoverable, next season's trade is unknowable, and the empty unit will still be there next year. Put those three together and watching one more season has real value.
2.1Timing of Investment and the Value of Waiting

The first titled section. It states the paper's whole idea in words, no maths: three features shared by most investment decisions combine to make waiting valuable.

2.2Three features are common to most investment decisions, and they combine to yield effects like those in the examples above.

The recipe has three ingredients, and together they produce the slow entries and stubborn stays of Section 1.

2.3First, almost as a matter of definition, an investment entails some sunk cost, an expenditure that cannot be recouped if the action is reversed at a later date.

Ingredient one: a sunk cost (money gone for good once spent; nothing comes back on reversal). If you later undo the investment, this money does not come back. Dixit says 'almost as a matter of definition' because spending you could fully recover would hardly be an investment at all.

2.4Second, the economic environment has ongoing uncertainty, and information arrives gradually.

Ingredient two: the world keeps rolling dice. You never know everything, but you keep learning: each season that passes tells you a little more.

2.5Finally, an investment opportunity does not generally disappear if not taken immediately; the decision is not only whether to invest, but also when to invest.

Ingredient three: the chance usually keeps. The question is not a one-off yes-or-no; it is a matter of timing. WHEN is a choice variable.

Key point
Memorise the recipe: (1) part of the cost is sunk; (2) uncertainty keeps arriving; (3) the opportunity waits for you. All three are needed. One of the paper's later qualifications (Section 5) is exactly a case where ingredient three is missing: a race for a scarce opportunity. The others are extra forces that can outweigh waiting.
2.6The qualitative implication is easily stated.

What follows from the recipe, in words rather than numbers, is simple.

2.7When these three conditions are present, waiting has positive value.

The paper's thesis sentence: with all three ingredients, doing nothing for now is worth something. Waiting is not dithering; it is an asset.

2.8In the evolving environment, time brings more information about the future prospects of the project.

Why: because ingredient two keeps paying out. Every week you wait, you observe more of how trade is going, and decide with better knowledge.

2.9As long as the opportunity to invest remains available, a later decision can be a better one.

Because of ingredient three, that better-informed decision is still yours to take. What waiting costs you is the profit you forgo meanwhile (2.11); the next sentence adds why acting early is risky.

2.10And because there are sunk costs, it does not always pay to take a less perfect action now and change it later.

And ingredient one blocks the 'just try it and undo it' escape. Undoing burns the sunk cost, so acting now on poor information is a real gamble, not a free experiment.

2.11Of course, the value of waiting must be set against the sacrifice of current profit.

The other side of the ledger: while you wait, an open shop would have been earning. Waiting costs you the profits not collected.

2.12If current conditions become sufficiently favorable, one should eventually take the action that is optimal according to the current calculation, and not wait any longer.

So waiting is not for ever. When today's conditions get good enough, the sacrifice outweighs the caution, and the right move is to act.

2.13But the "trigger" level of currently expected profit that makes it optimal to proceed exceeds the Marshallian normal return.

But, and this is the punchline, the trigger (a level of revenue at which a decision fires: the investment trigger fires when revenue rises to it, the abandonment trigger when revenue falls to it; on the near side of each, you wait) at which acting becomes right sits above Marshall's break-even. The bar is higher than the textbook says.

2.14Similarly, waiting has value when contemplating disinvestment.

The same logic runs in reverse for quitting: before you close a losing operation, waiting to see whether trade recovers also has value.

2.15The Marshallian criterion of failing to cover variable cost should not trigger abandonment; the correct point is a critical negative level of operating profit.

So Marshall's exit rule is wrong too. Do not close merely because you fail to cover running costs; close only when losses reach some definite depth. The right exit trigger is a level of loss, not the break-even line.

2.16This view of investment under uncertainty can be summarized as "a theory of optimal inertia," or "a benevolent tyranny of the status quo."

Two nicknames for the theory: doing nothing is often optimal ('optimal inertia'), and the way things already are gets a built-in advantage, a tyranny of the status quo (the way things currently are), but a kindly one, because it serves the decision-maker's own interest.

4.25I have set the trend growth rate of revenues at zero in the above analysis, but this is a good place to mention its twofold effect.

Recall the zero-trend assumption from Section 3. Dropping it has two opposing effects.

The story so far
Where we are: Meera's toy model has done its job. Now the paper asks what it left out. Could she open a bigger or smaller shop? 5.1Extensions and Qualifications

A section of caveats. 'Extensions' are ways the simple model can be enlarged; 'qualifications' are cases where its lesson weakens or reverses.

5.2The above example of a discrete investment project was deliberately oversimplified to highlight the value of waiting.

The paper admits that Section 3 was a cartoon on purpose. Simplicity made the value of waiting easy to see.

Real decisions bring complications, and some forces push the other way. 'Countervailing' means pulling in the opposite direction.

What follows is a short tour, not a full treatment.

5.7If the net revenues can sometimes become negative, we can allow temporary suspension or abandonment.

A fourth enlargement is the important one. If the project can lose money, the firm might pause it or scrap it. That is where the story goes next: 6.1.

5.8This last extension raises some interesting new issues, and is considered in the next section.

Shutting down raises fresh questions, so it gets a section of its own, Section 6.

5.9The assumption that net revenues follow a Brownian motion embodies a restriction: uncertainty is roughly symmetric around the trend.

Now a hidden assumption. Brownian motion (primer P6) makes good and bad surprises equally likely around the trend (the steady tilt in the path). That is a symmetric (balanced: up and down equally likely) world.

5.10In practice, distributions of future outcomes are sometimes quite lopsided.

Reality is often not balanced. Some risks have a small chance of a disaster and a large chance of little change, or the reverse.

5.11Therefore, we should know what they do to investment decisions.

So the question is what unbalanced risk does to the decision.

5.12Bernanke (1983) found the answer and called it the bad news principle: "of possible future outcomes, only the unfavorable ones have a bearing on the current propensity to undertake a given project."

Bernanke worked this out in 1983 (R.3) and gave it a memorable name, the bad news principle (Bernanke's finding that, when a firm can wait, only the possible bad outcomes affect whether it invests now; the size of possible good outcomes does not, because waiting keeps them anyway). The quoted words are Bernanke's own: only the unfavourable possibilities bear on the decision to invest today.

Note on the original

The phrase 'bad news principle' is set in italics in the print, marking it as a coined term.

Key point
The bad news principle is the paper's second big idea, after the value of waiting itself. It says which half of the uncertainty matters. If you can wait, the upside will still be there tomorrow; only the downside can be dodged by waiting. So the downside, together with the total chance of ending up above the trigger, sets the value of waiting; the shape of the upside does not.
5.13In other words, the downside risk is the primary force governing optimal investment decisions when waiting is possible.

Plain version: when waiting is possible, the downside risk (the chance of bad outcomes) is what drives the investment decision.

5.14To be more precise, the total upside probability matters, but not the shape of the distribution of revenues to the right of the optimal trigger.

A refinement. The total probability of ending up above the trigger still matters. But how that upside is shaped, whether a modest gain or a jackpot, makes no difference to the decision to invest today.

5.15The technical proof of this is in an additional Appendix available from the author.

The proof is in the same unpublished 'additional Appendix' mentioned in 3.18 and 3.61, available by post.

5.16But the intuition is not difficult.

But you do not need the proof to see why it is true.

5.17Remember that when we decide whether to proceed or to wait a little more, what is at stake is not the uncertainty per se, but how it will resolve in the next small amount of time; that is, the tradeoff between current and future risks.

The decision is never 'invest or never invest'. It is 'invest now or look again shortly'. So what matters is not how uncertain the far future is, but how the next short stretch of time might change your mind. That is the trade-off between risk now and risk later.

5.18Most of the upside potential remains whether action occurs right away, or after a small delay.

If the good outcome is coming, a short delay costs you little of it. The upside survives the wait.

5.19The possibility of a downturn, and the ability to avoid an action that could thereby prove to be a mistake, is what makes waiting valuable.

The downside is different. If bad news is about to arrive, a short wait lets you avoid a mistake you could not undo. That escape is the whole value of waiting.

5.20That is why the downside risk matters most when deciding whether to wait.

So the downside is what counts when you decide whether to wait. In the story: Meera watches the weather forecast for storms, not for sunshine. Sunshine will still be there if she opens next month.

A quick comparison
Think of buying a second-hand car you can inspect again next week. Whether it might turn out even better than it looks hardly matters: if so, it will still be good next week. Whether it might have a hidden fault matters a great deal, because a week of checks could save you from a purchase you cannot return. Only the bad news drives the decision to wait.
5.21Let us turn to some other issues that were left out of the model.

Change of topic: things the model left out altogether.

5.22In the simple example, waiting had a positive value because it allowed further observations of the revenue fluctuations.

In the toy model, waiting was valuable for one reason only: it let you watch revenue move a little longer.

5.23More generally, the point is that the passage of time reveals more information.

The general point is broader: time brings information of every kind, not just the next revenue figure.

5.24In reality there are other forces that also bear on the issue of whether to wait.

And other forces bear on the timing decision too. Three are about to be listed: a race, a strategic advantage in moving first, and learning from what rivals do.

5.25First, there may be a race to seize a scarce opportunity.

Complication one: a race. If the opportunity is scarce and several firms want it, someone else may take it while you wait.

5.26In the simple example, the opportunity to invest was assigned to just one firm.

In Section 3 the chance to invest belonged to one firm alone, as footnote 7 (3.74) warned.

5.27But if it is available to any of several firms, then waiting is no longer feasible.

Open the opportunity to several firms and waiting stops being an option, because the chance may vanish.

5.28The option to wait will expire because some competitor will seize the opportunity.

The option value (the worth of keeping a choice open) of waiting dies when a rival can end the option by moving first.

5.29Then some firm will invest as soon as the expected present value crosses zero, and the Marshallian trigger will be valid.

So in a race, somebody invests the moment the project's net worth (present value of earnings minus set-up cost) turns positive. That is the old Marshallian rule from 3.38, back in force. In the story: if a dozen savers can sign for the unit, the first to see takings cross break-even signs, and the card saying 'open only at £H' never gets written.

Watch out
This is the sharpest limit on the paper's argument, and the paper says so itself. The value of waiting needs an opportunity that keeps. Where the opportunity can be snatched, Marshall was right after all. Keep this in mind when the paper turns to whole industries in Section 8, where it finds a subtler answer.
5.30But when there are several firms, a more interesting scenario is that more than one firm can invest.

A more interesting case: not a race for one prize, but a market where many firms can all build.

5.31When they do so, industry supply increases and price falls along the demand curve.

When several build, the industry produces more, and the extra output pushes the price down the demand (how much buyers want at each price) curve (primer P3).

5.32This, or the expectation of such price fall, places a limit on the equilibrium investment.

The falling price, or the mere expectation of it, caps how much investment the industry as a whole will make.

5.33In other words, we have a competitive industry in a dynamic environment.

That is the definition of a competition (many small firms, none able to move the price alone)-style industry, now placed in a world that keeps changing.

5.34This is the natural setting in which to explore the validity of the Marshallian story of entry at long run average cost and exit at short run variable cost.

This is the right arena for testing Marshall's two rules, because those rules were written for exactly such an industry: enter when price covers long run average cost (full cost per unit including a return on the set-up money), leave when it falls below average variable cost (running cost per unit only).

Complication three: firms may know different things.

A worked case. Each firm forms its own estimate of how good the project is, and each estimate is a bit wrong.

If you see nobody else building, you conclude their estimates must have been poor, and you mark your own down.

5.44When all firms do this, they may all decide to wait.

Real firms do lose money on their day-to-day operations, so the model must allow it.

Two changes of meaning. Two simplifications to keep the algebra short: no underlying drift in revenue, and a running cost that never changes.

But pausing is not the same as disinvestment (pulling out of an investment for good). The textbook slides between the two.

6.48Optimal Inertia

A subheading for the idea the paper flagged in Section 2: standing still is often the best move.

The story so far

Key point
Hysteresis is the title's second word and the paper's most quoted idea. Its recipe is the zone of inaction: because there is a wide band where nothing changes, a push that crosses one edge of the band is not undone by a return to the middle. You would have to cross the other edge to undo it.
Background: the physics behind the borrowed word
The word comes from the Greek for 'lagging behind'. In a magnet, tiny regions of the iron line up when a current flows; when it stops, many stay lined up because turning back costs energy. Plot magnetisation against current and you get a loop, not a single line: the path up differs from the path down. Economists borrowed the word in the 1980s for unemployment that stayed high after the recession that caused it had ended, and this paper borrows it for investment.

The list as a whole

Who wrote them. Twenty-nine works. Six are by Dixit himself, a fifth of the list, and the whole of the Princeton circle is well represented: Leahy (two entries) was a Princeton doctoral student; Bernanke, Blinder, Grossman and Svensson thanked in the acknowledgements (10.9) were colleagues or visitors. Two more pairs recur: McDonald and Siegel (two entries) and Pindyck (two entries), the other founders of the field. Krugman appears twice. No empirical study with a dataset appears at all: the evidence in the paper is anecdotal or drawn from other people's charts.

Where they appeared. Five items are in the Quarterly Journal of Economics and three in the Journal of Political Economy, the two most prestigious general journals of the day. The rest are scattered across a dozen outlets. Six of the twenty-nine had not been published at all when this paper went to press: four working papers, one doctoral thesis and one RAND report. Of those six, two later appeared in journals (Leahy in 1993, and very probably Lippman and Rumelt in 1992 under a new title), one became a short book (Dixit's Art of Smooth Pasting, 1993), and one appeared in an edited volume (Dixit's 1991b paper, in 1993); the RAND report and the doctoral thesis, as far as this guide could trace, were never published as such.

When they appeared. The oldest item is Merton (1973); the next oldest, Hamermesh and Soss (1974). Then a gap until Dixit (1980). Twenty-four of the twenty-nine are dated 1985 or later, and fourteen are dated 1989 or later. This is the reference list of a field that was about six years old.

Does the pattern matter? Yes, in three ways. First, the paper is a survey by a founder of the field, so it is partly an advertisement for a research programme; you should read its confidence with that in mind, as the paper itself half admits at 1.32. Second, the heavy reliance on unpublished work means several claims rested, in 1992, on results that had not yet been through peer review; the epilogue reports which of them were later published. Third, the absence of any dataset explains why the paper's numbers are all 'illustrative calculations' (2.20) rather than measurements. None of this makes the argument wrong. It tells you what kind of document you are reading: a persuasive tour by an insider, written early.

Accuracy of the list. Every entry was checked against library records. Six contain small errors, all flagged above: a misspelt author (R.5), a misquoted title (R.13), two wrong page ranges (R.16, R.18), a truncated title with a wrong final page (R.22), and a wrong volume number (R.27). That is seven slips in twenty-nine entries. None affects the argument; all would send a reader to the wrong shelf.

EEpilogue: what happened after 1992

Separate from the paper

Everything before this point explains the paper as written in late 1991. This section reports what happened afterwards and scores the paper against the record. Every figure carries a source from the numbered list at the end, each checked on 31 August 2026; where something could not be checked, it says so.

E1. The idea conquered the field

Two years after this article, Dixit and Robert Pindyck published the book version, Investment under Uncertainty (Princeton University Press, January 1994) [T1]. It became the field's standard reference: one citation database counted about 4,700 citations of it by 2026 [T2]. The field took the name real options (analysing real investment with the tools of financial options): Trigeorgis's Real Options followed in 1996 [T3], and the leading corporate-finance textbook, Brealey and Myers's Principles of Corporate Finance, now carries a chapter called 'Real Options' [T4]. The 'additional Appendix, available by writing to the author' became quaint within a few years of the web arriving.

Dixit himself retired from teaching in 2010 and remains Princeton's John J. F. Sherrerd '52 University Professor of Economics Emeritus. He was President of the Econometric Society in 2001 and of the American Economic Association in 2008, and in 2016 India awarded him the Padma Vibhushan, its second-highest civilian honour [T5]. Princeton now awards a student prize in his name [T6].

E2. Did managers listen?

Only partly, but the paper's prediction about behaviour held up strikingly. A large 2001 survey of American chief financial officers found that more than a quarter said they used real-options methods when judging projects, while the great majority still relied on net present value and internal rate of return [T7]. A 2007 survey of large firms reported explicit use to be rarer still; a figure of about 14 per cent is often quoted from it, but this guide could not consult the article and does not vouch for the number [T8]. So the technique stayed a minority practice.

The behaviour, though, matched the paper exactly. Summers's median hurdle rate of 15 per cent (1.7) refused to fall for three decades while interest rates collapsed to zero. A 2016 study in the Journal of Financial Economics found firms' discount rates averaging about twice their cost of financial capital [T9]. The Reserve Bank of Australia reported in 2021 that median hurdle rates sat near 13 per cent against a cost of capital near 6 per cent, and pointed to the value of waiting under irreversibility as one explanation [T10]. A Federal Reserve survey study found business investment 'extremely insensitive' to falls in interest rates [T11]. A 2025 paper in the American Economic Review found that hurdle rates track the cost of capital far less than one for one and adjust only over many years [T32]. That is the world of sentences 4.35 and 4.36: option-value reasoning, whether or not managers call it that.

E3. Uncertainty economics grew into an industry

The paper's mechanism, uncertainty freezing investment, became one of the busiest areas of macroeconomics. Nicholas Bloom's 'The Impact of Uncertainty Shocks' (Econometrica, 2009) showed spikes of uncertainty producing the wait-and-see slumps this paper predicts [T12]; Baker, Bloom and Davis built a newspaper-based index of policy uncertainty that is now watched worldwide (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2016) [T13]. Britain supplied a natural experiment: after the June 2016 Brexit referendum, with the trading rules unknown for years, a study using the Bank of England's Decision Maker Panel estimated UK business investment about 11 per cent lower over three years than it would otherwise have been [T14]. Firms did what Meera does: they waited. In 2025 Bloom was named a Clarivate Citation Laureate for this body of work [T33].

E4. The cast went on to bigger things

Ben Bernanke, whose bad news principle the paper leans on (5.12), chaired the Federal Reserve from February 2006 to January 2014, through the financial crisis [T16], and shared the 2022 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in economics with Diamond and Dybvig for research on banks and financial crises [T17]. The name 'bad news principle' survived in later research papers [T18]. McDonald and Siegel's 1986 paper (R.24) is now recognised as the founding calculation of the field; Pindyck's 1991 survey (R.27) remains a standard reading-list item; and Leahy's 1991 working paper (R.20) appeared in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 1993 under a title that states the result of 8.58 outright: 'Investment in Competitive Equilibrium: The Optimality of Myopic Behavior' [T19].

E5. The U.S. v.s. Japan section aged strangely

Dixit wrote 7.16 just as Japan's great asset bubble was bursting: share prices had peaked at the end of 1989 and land prices in 1990 [T20]. The Japan of 1992, aggressive investors hanging on through losses, sank into the 'lost decades'. Banks propped up loss-making 'zombie' firms rather than let them exit, and later research treats that refusal to disinvest as a cause of Japan's stagnation (Caballero, Hoshi and Kashyap, American Economic Review, 2008) [T21]. Read through this paper's lens the diagnosis holds together uncomfortably well: a protected downside did delay exit, and it turned out to be possible to have far too little of the discipline that exit provides. Lifetime employment, the quasi-fixed labour of 7.13, survived for core male workers but shrank at the edges as non-regular work grew [T22]. The American manufacturing pessimism of Made in America reads today as a period piece: the sectors named in 7.11 were indeed lost or transformed, but the wider prophecy of American industrial decline was overtaken by the 1990s technology boom.

E6. Hysteresis went macro

On the paper's own example, US imports and the 1980s dollar, the micro evidence backed the sunk-cost story. Baldwin's 'beachhead effect' paper (1988) and Baldwin and Krugman (1989) formalised it, and Roberts and Tybout found sunk-cost hysteresis in firm-level export data (1997) [T23]. At the whole-economy level the picture is more mixed: after the Plaza Accord of September 1985 pushed the dollar down, the US trade deficit went on widening until the third quarter of 1987 and then fell to about 30 billion dollars by 1991, so adjustment came, with long lags [T24]. The word hysteresis, meanwhile, escaped into macroeconomics at large: borrowed for lasting unemployment damage (Blanchard and Summers, 1986), revived after 2008 as 'scarring', the finding that recessions leave permanent marks on output, and surveyed in 2023 under the plain title 'Hysteresis and Business Cycles' [T25].

E8. Corrections to the paper's reference list

Checking every reference against library records turned up several slips in the paper's own list, all small, all real [T30]. The Pindyck survey (R.27) appeared in volume 29 of the Journal of Economic Literature, not 26. The Mankiw article (R.22) ends at page 538 (some indexes say 537), not 539, and its full title ends '...Model of Monopoly'. Hamermesh and Soss (R.16) runs to page 98, not 90. The Frankel and Meese title (R.13) is 'Are Exchange Rates Excessively Variable?', not 'Too Variable'. Krugman and Baldwin (R.18) runs to page 43 as an article and to page 56 with the printed discussion, not 44. And the lead author spelt 'Dertouzas' throughout (R.5) is Michael L. Dertouzos. None of these changes any argument; they are listed so you can find the works.

E9. Small updates to period details

The Journal of Economic Perspectives has been free to read online since 2011, so the paper itself is one click away [T26]. GAUSS (X.70), the number-crunching software of the appendix, is still sold by Aptech Systems, which released version 26 in February 2026 [T27]. New York City still has rent-controlled flats, 24,020 of them at the 2023 count, so the closing anecdote (9.14) still works [T28]. And the economics of suicide begun by Hamermesh and Soss (9.7) grew into a serious empirical literature, surveyed in the Journal of Economic Surveys in 2012 [T29].

VVerification appendix: what was checked and what was corrected

This appendix reports what was checked, how, what was found wrong, and what was done about it, including the mistakes made while preparing this guide. Sections V1 to V8 follow the order of the brief.

V1. Reproduction and coverage

The source is the American Economic Association's scan of the journal pages with a publisher's text layer. That text layer was read by script (pdftotext with word-position output), never retyped. The script stripped running headers, joined hyphenated line breaks (keeping the hyphen in three real compounds: no-entry-no-exit, probability-weighted, rent-controlled), turned raised footnote markers, superscripts and subscripts into markup, joined lines into paragraphs across page breaks, split paragraphs into sentences with a list of protected abbreviations, placed each footnote after the sentence carrying its marker, and numbered every item by position within its section. Displayed equations were located by their neighbouring lines and typed from the page images, because the text layer garbles them and omits five of them altogether: equations (2), (3), (6) and (7) and the appendix display for the adjusted discount rate.

Item counts, all checked against the page images: 622 numbered items in total; 501 body sentences; 43 footnote sentences in 13 footnotes; 3 italic notes (the author note and two acknowledgement sentences); masthead, title and author line; 17 section headings and subheadings (the references heading is unnumbered); 24 displayed equations; 3 figure captions; 29 reference entries. The build script confirms that every one of the 622 items has a rewrite, that none is duplicated, and that the rewrites appear in the paper's order; it also refuses to build if a term is defined twice, a cross-reference points nowhere, or a safe-to-skip zone is left open.

Splitting was checked by reading the complete list of 622 items against the page images. Six faults in the automatic splitting were found and fixed in the script before the guide was written: the abbreviation list was matching inside words (so 'observed.' and 'stated.' were treated like 'ed.' and not split); a capital letter followed by a full stop was protected as an initial everywhere, which glued sentences ending in a variable such as 'R.' to the next one, so the protection was restricted to the author note and references; a raised footnote marker between the full stop and the next sentence prevented a split; raised exponents such as the 2 in σ² were being read as footnote markers, so markers were required to follow a full stop or closing bracket and to be used once; the drop-cap T on the first page was read as a separate word; and figure captions at the top of a page were breaking the sentence that runs across that page. The item list was then reviewed a second time in full and no further splitting faults were found.

V4. Independent review of the definitions

All 167 definitions were sent as a bare numbered list, without the surrounding text, to an independent reviewer asked to find definitions that were wrong, misleading, contradictory or circular. The reviewer reported 14 problems. Thirteen were accepted and the definitions rewritten; one (a request to merge two entries for the Journal of Economic Perspectives) was handled by deleting the duplicate. The most important corrections: 'value of waiting' had been defined as the option's time value, whereas the paper uses it for the whole value of the opportunity while waiting, which is what makes smooth pasting a statement about that curve; 'trigger' had described only the investment trigger; 'fixed cost' had wrongly made escapability part of the definition; 'value matching' was circular and omitted the sunk cost; 'variance' did not say it averages squared distances; 'Brownian motion' was named after pollen rather than Robert Brown; 'real options' wrongly implied the name arose after this paper (Stewart Myers coined it in 1977); 'marginal product of labour' equated an output with a wage; and 'quasi-fixed factor', 'rational expectations', 'signalling game', 'predatory dumping' and 'non-homogeneous' were each made more exact. The list here was regenerated after the fixes.
